# Why Your Gaming PC Gets Slower Over Time (and How to Restore Lost FPS)

You're not imagining it. That PC that used to tear through every game at 120+ FPS now struggles to hold 80. The menus feel sluggier, boot times have crept up from 12 seconds to 45, and games you played smoothly a year ago now stutter in busy scenes.

This is one of the most common complaints we see from users running scans through our [free bottleneck analyzer](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store): "My hardware hasn't changed, but my performance has dropped." The hardware didn't get weaker — but a dozen things around it quietly changed, each shaving off a few percent of performance until the cumulative loss becomes impossible to ignore.

Here's every reason it happens, and exactly how to claw that performance back.

---

## The Two Types of PC Slowdown

Before diving into fixes, understand that performance degradation falls into two buckets:

1. **Your PC is doing more work than it used to.** Software changes — accumulated background processes, heavier OS updates, bloated drivers — mean your hardware is spending cycles on things that aren't your game.
2. **Your hardware isn't performing at its original spec.** Thermal degradation, dust accumulation, and storage wear physically reduce what your components can deliver.

Most PCs suffering from slowdown have problems in both categories. We'll cover each one and show you how to measure whether it's affecting you.

---

## 1. Startup Programs Are Eating Your CPU Alive

Every app you install asks to run at startup. After a year of normal use, the average Windows gaming PC we see in scan data has 25–40 startup programs — up from 8–12 on a fresh install. Each one claims "just 1–2% CPU," but together they consume 15–25% of your processor before you even launch a game.

The worst offenders accumulate silently: game launchers (Steam, Epic, GOG, Battle.net, EA App each running background services), RGB control software (iCUE, Synapse, Armoury Crate, SignalRGB), hardware monitoring tools, cloud sync clients, and auto-updaters for apps you forgot you installed.

**How to fix it:**

1. Press `Ctrl+Shift+Esc` → Startup tab
2. Sort by "Startup impact"
3. Disable everything that isn't your antivirus or audio driver
4. Reboot and re-enable only what you actually miss

**Expected recovery:** 5–15% CPU headroom reclaimed. On a CPU-bottlenecked system, that translates directly to 5–15% more FPS.

---

## 2. Windows Updates Have Made Your OS Heavier

Windows 10 in 2020 and Windows 11 in 2026 are not the same operating system, even on the same hardware. Each feature update adds telemetry services, new background processes, and occasionally changes to the scheduler that affect gaming performance. The cumulative effect after two to three years of updates is measurable: we've seen 3–8% performance overhead from OS bloat alone in controlled before-and-after testing.

Particularly impactful changes include:
- **VBS (Virtualization-Based Security)** — enabled by default in newer Windows 11 builds, costs 5–15% in CPU-sensitive games
- **Memory Integrity / HVCI** — another security feature that adds overhead to every kernel call
- **Widgets and News feeds** — background web rendering that consumes CPU and RAM even when you never open them

**How to fix it:**

- Disable VBS if you don't need enterprise-level security: Settings → Privacy & Security → Windows Security → Device Security → Core Isolation → toggle off Memory Integrity, then reboot
- Disable Widgets: Settings → Personalization → Taskbar → toggle off Widgets
- Run `services.msc` and set "Connected User Experiences and Telemetry" to Disabled
- Disable "Tips and Suggestions" notifications in Settings → System → Notifications

**Expected recovery:** 3–10% depending on how many features were silently enabled. VBS alone can account for most of this on CPU-bound titles.

## 3. Your Thermal Paste Has Degraded

Thermal paste isn't permanent. The compound between your CPU and cooler (and your GPU die and heatsink) gradually dries out, cracks, and loses thermal conductivity. Cheap paste can degrade noticeably in 12–18 months. Even quality compounds like Noctua NT-H1 or Thermal Grizzly Kryonaut show measurable degradation after 3–5 years.

When thermal paste degrades, heat transfer drops. Your CPU or GPU hits its thermal throttle point sooner, reduces clock speeds to protect itself, and your frame rate drops — especially during sustained gaming sessions.

**How to diagnose it:**

Run a stress test (Prime95 for CPU, FurMark for GPU) and monitor temperatures with HWiNFO64. Compare your results to what reviewers got with your exact cooler and chip. If your CPU is hitting 90°C+ under load when reviewers showed 75°C with the same setup, thermal paste degradation is likely.

**How to fix it:**

1. Remove your CPU cooler
2. Clean off old paste with 90%+ isopropyl alcohol and a lint-free cloth
3. Apply fresh thermal paste — a pea-sized dot in the center for most CPUs
4. Remount the cooler with even pressure

For GPUs, the process is similar but requires removing the heatsink shroud (look up a teardown for your specific card). If you're not comfortable with GPU disassembly, a repaste service at a local PC shop typically costs $30–$50.

**Expected recovery:** 5–15°C temperature drop, which eliminates thermal throttling. If your CPU was throttling from 5.0 GHz down to 4.2 GHz under heat, that's a significant FPS recovery — sometimes 10–20% in thermally-limited scenarios.

## 4. Dust Is Choking Your Cooling

Dust buildup is the silent performance killer. Dust on heatsink fins acts as insulation, reducing cooling capacity by 10–30% after 12–18 months in a typical room. Dust-clogged intake fans can cut airflow by half, and a GPU with blocked fin stacks will thermal throttle during any sustained gaming session.

The worst-case scenario is dust between your GPU's heatsink fins — you can't see it without removing the shroud, and it accumulates faster than you'd expect, especially if you have pets or carpet near your PC.

**How to fix it:**

1. Shut down and unplug your PC
2. Take it outside or to a well-ventilated area
3. Use compressed air (short bursts, hold fans still to prevent them spinning) to blow dust from heatsink fins, fan blades, PSU intake, and mesh filters
4. Clean dust filters with water and let them dry completely before reinstalling
5. Repeat every 3–4 months, or monthly if you have pets

**Expected recovery:** If your components were thermal throttling from dust buildup, cleaning can recover 10–25% of lost performance. Even without throttling, lower temps mean higher sustained boost clocks.

## 5. Your SSD Is Full (and That's Slowing Everything Down)

SSDs lose performance as they fill up. This isn't a myth — it's a physical consequence of how NAND flash works. When an SSD is 90%+ full, the controller has fewer empty blocks to write to, can't effectively use its SLC cache, and must perform more garbage collection operations. The result: write speeds drop by 50–80%, and random I/O (which games depend on for asset loading and shader caches) degrades significantly.

On a 1TB NVMe drive at 95% capacity, sequential writes can drop from 5,000 MB/s to under 1,000 MB/s. Random 4K performance — the metric that actually matters for game loading — can drop by 40–60%.

**How to fix it:**

- Keep at least 15–20% of your drive free. On a 1TB drive, that means keeping 150–200 GB available
- Move games you're not actively playing to a secondary drive or uninstall them
- Clear your Windows temp folder: press `Win+R`, type `%temp%`, delete everything inside
- Run Disk Cleanup as administrator to remove old Windows update files (can free 10–30 GB)
- Check for and delete old shader cache files in `%localappdata%` — these can grow to several GB per game

**Expected recovery:** Restored SSD write performance and faster game load times. In games with heavy asset streaming (open-world titles, UE5 games), this can also reduce texture pop-in and traversal stutters.

## 6. Your Drivers Are Stale or Bloated

GPU driver updates are a double-edged sword. New drivers add optimizations for new games but can sometimes regress performance in older titles. More importantly, repeatedly installing driver updates over the old ones — the default behavior for both NVIDIA and AMD installers — leaves behind registry entries, old shader caches, and configuration fragments that accumulate over years.

We've seen users with 3+ years of layered GPU driver installs whose shader compilation stuttering was 2–3x worse than a fresh driver install on the same hardware.

**How to fix it:**

1. Download the latest driver for your GPU from NVIDIA or AMD's website
2. Download [DDU (Display Driver Uninstaller)](https://www.guru3d.com/download/display-driver-uninstaller-download/)
3. Boot into Safe Mode
4. Run DDU to completely remove all GPU driver traces
5. Reboot normally and install the fresh driver

Also update your chipset drivers from your motherboard manufacturer's support page — outdated chipset drivers can cause USB latency issues and CPU scheduling problems that affect frame times.

**Expected recovery:** Reduced shader compilation stuttering, 2–5% FPS improvement in some titles, and elimination of driver-related crashes or graphical glitches.

## 7. TRIM Isn't Running (and Your SSD Is Silently Dying)

TRIM is the command that tells your SSD which data blocks are no longer in use and can be wiped for new writes. Without TRIM, deleted files leave behind "dirty" blocks that the SSD controller must clean up during writes — adding latency to every storage operation.

TRIM should run automatically in Windows, but it can be disabled by certain storage drivers, third-party disk tools, or BIOS settings (particularly if your SATA controller is set to IDE mode instead of AHCI).

**How to check:**

Open Command Prompt as administrator and run:

```
fsutil behavior query DisableDeleteNotify
```

If the result shows `DisableDeleteNotify = 0`, TRIM is enabled. If it shows `1`, TRIM is disabled and you need to fix it.

**How to fix it:**

```
fsutil behavior set DisableDeleteNotify 0
```

Then force a manual TRIM:

```
defrag C: /O
```

If your BIOS is set to IDE mode and you installed Windows that way, switching to AHCI requires a registry edit before changing the BIOS setting — search "switch IDE to AHCI without reinstalling Windows" for a step-by-step guide specific to your Windows version.

**Expected recovery:** Restored SSD write performance and consistent I/O latency. The difference is most noticeable in game loading times and open-world texture streaming.

## 8. Game Patches Made the Games Heavier

This one isn't your PC's fault at all. Games themselves get more demanding after launch. Post-release patches add higher-quality assets, new visual effects, ray tracing enhancements, and expanded game worlds that require more CPU, GPU, and RAM than the launch version.

Cyberpunk 2077 at launch ran roughly 15–20% faster than the current version with all content updates at the same settings. Fortnite in 2026 uses 40% more RAM and requires 25% more CPU than Fortnite in 2023. This is normal, but it means the FPS you got at launch isn't guaranteed to hold.

**How to deal with it:**

- Drop one quality preset level. Going from Ultra to High typically costs 5–10% visual quality but gains 20–40% FPS
- Disable ray tracing if you have a mid-range GPU. RT is the first thing to sacrifice when a game gets heavier through patches
- Use DLSS, FSR, or XeSS upscaling to recover frames without reducing visual quality dramatically
- Check game-specific subreddits for optimized settings guides — the community usually identifies which settings give the worst performance per visual quality

**Expected recovery:** Varies per game, but dropping from Ultra to High with upscaling enabled can recover 30–50% of frame rate at minimal visual cost.

## 9. You Never Enabled XMP/EXPO (or It Got Reset)

This is one of the most common issues we catch with our analyzer, and it affects both new and aging PCs. When you buy DDR4-3600 or DDR5-6000 RAM, it ships running at the base JEDEC speed — DDR4-2133 or DDR5-4800. You must manually enable XMP (Intel) or EXPO (AMD) in your BIOS to get the speed you paid for.

Here's the kicker: BIOS updates can reset XMP/EXPO to disabled. If your motherboard auto-updated its BIOS, or if you cleared CMOS to fix an unrelated issue, your RAM may have silently reverted to base speed. You'd never notice in everyday use — but in games, the difference between DDR4-2133 and DDR4-3600 is 10–15% FPS, and the gap is even larger with DDR5.

**How to check:**

Open Task Manager → Performance → Memory. Look at the "Speed" field. If it shows 2133 MHz for DDR4 or 4800 MHz for DDR5, XMP/EXPO is not active.

**How to fix it:**

1. Reboot into BIOS (usually by pressing Delete or F2 during boot)
2. Find the XMP/EXPO/DOCP setting (location varies by motherboard manufacturer)
3. Enable Profile 1
4. Save and exit

**Expected recovery:** 10–20% FPS improvement in CPU-bound scenarios. This is free performance you already paid for.

## 10. Malware or Cryptominers Are Stealing Your Resources

If your PC suddenly started performing worse without any obvious change, consider that unwanted software may be consuming resources in the background. Cryptomining malware is particularly insidious — it runs your GPU or CPU at high utilization when you're not gaming, and some variants throttle themselves when you launch a game to avoid detection, only to resume when you close it.

**Signs to watch for:**
- High GPU usage when idle (check Task Manager → Performance → GPU)
- Unexplained CPU usage above 20–30% when nothing is running
- Fans running loud during idle or light tasks
- Unfamiliar processes in Task Manager, especially ones with random alphanumeric names

**How to fix it:**

1. Run a full scan with [Malwarebytes](https://www.malwarebytes.com/) (free version is sufficient for detection)
2. Check Task Manager for suspicious processes and research any you don't recognize
3. Review your browser extensions — malicious extensions can run cryptominers in background tabs
4. If you find malware, consider a clean Windows reinstall for complete peace of mind

## The Nuclear Option: Clean Windows Install

If you've had the same Windows installation for 3+ years, accumulated dozens of driver installs, hundreds of programs installed and uninstalled, and multiple major Windows feature updates, sometimes the most effective fix is starting fresh. A clean Windows install eliminates:

- All accumulated startup programs
- Leftover registry entries from old software
- Broken driver chains
- Corrupted system files
- Hidden malware

After a clean install, you'll need to reinstall your drivers, games, and applications — but the performance difference can be dramatic. We've seen users recover 15–25% of their original performance from a clean install alone, even with no hardware changes.

**Before you reinstall:**
- Back up your game saves (check `%userprofile%\Documents\My Games\` and `%appdata%`)
- Export your browser bookmarks and passwords
- Note your Windows license key (`wmic path softwarelicensingservice get OA3xOriginalProductKey` in an admin Command Prompt)
- Back up any files on your C: drive you want to keep

## The Full Restoration Checklist

Do everything in this order for maximum impact:

1. **Scan for malware** with Malwarebytes
2. **Clean dust** from all heatsinks and fans
3. **Verify XMP/EXPO** is enabled in BIOS
4. **Disable VBS/HVCI** if you don't need enterprise security
5. **Clean install GPU drivers** with DDU
6. **Update chipset drivers** from motherboard manufacturer
7. **Clear startup programs** in Task Manager
8. **Free up SSD space** to at least 15–20% available
9. **Verify TRIM** is enabled
10. **Repaste CPU** (and GPU if temperatures are high)
11. **Optimize in-game settings** — drop from Ultra to High, enable upscaling
12. **Set Windows power plan** to High Performance for gaming sessions

If all of that still isn't enough, a clean Windows reinstall is the final step before considering a hardware upgrade.

## When It Really Is Time for New Hardware

Sometimes a PC genuinely has fallen behind what modern games demand. If you've done everything above and you're still struggling, the hardware itself may be the bottleneck — not degradation, but obsolescence. Signs that optimization can't save you:

- **CPU from 2019 or earlier** (Intel 9th gen, Ryzen 2000 series) paired with modern games that require strong single-thread performance
- **GPU with 4GB VRAM or less** in games that require 8GB+ at your target resolution
- **8GB system RAM** when modern games routinely use 12–16 GB
- **HDD as your primary game drive** — no amount of optimization fixes mechanical drive latency
