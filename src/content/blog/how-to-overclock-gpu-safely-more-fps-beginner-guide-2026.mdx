# How to Overclock Your GPU Safely for More FPS: Beginner's Guide (2026)

Your graphics card is leaving performance on the table. Every GPU ships with clock speeds set conservatively by the manufacturer — frequencies that need to work reliably across millions of chips with varying silicon quality, across every case airflow scenario, across every power supply on the market. Your specific card, in your specific case, with your specific cooling? It can almost certainly run faster.

GPU overclocking pushes your card's clock speeds and memory speeds beyond their factory defaults, extracting 5–15% more FPS for free. No new hardware. No cost. Just 30 minutes of your time and a free piece of software.

If overclocking sounds risky, here's the reality in 2026: modern GPUs have extensive hardware-level protections. They'll throttle, crash the driver, or shut down entirely before any physical damage occurs. The absolute worst case is a screen freeze that requires a restart. You cannot blow up your GPU by moving sliders in MSI Afterburner.

We've analyzed over 50,000 system scans through our [PC Bottleneck Analyzer](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store), and a significant portion of systems are running GPUs at stock speeds that have thermal headroom to spare. If your GPU stays below 80°C during gaming, you're a strong candidate for overclocking.

> - GPU overclocking increases your core clock and memory clock speeds beyond factory defaults, delivering 5–15% more FPS — for free.
> - **Tools needed:** MSI Afterburner (free) for NVIDIA, AMD Adrenalin (built-in) for AMD Radeon cards.
> - **Is it safe?** Yes. Modern GPUs cannot be damaged by software overclocking. The worst case is a driver crash or freeze — just restart and dial back.

## GPU Overclocking vs CPU Overclocking: What's Different

If you've read our [CPU overclocking guide](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/how-to-overclock-cpu-safely-beginner-guide-free-fps-2026), you might expect GPU overclocking to work the same way. It doesn't — and in many ways, it's simpler.

**No BIOS required.** GPU overclocking is done entirely through software while Windows is running. No restarting into BIOS, no risk of a system that won't boot.

**Every GPU can be overclocked.** Unlike CPUs, where Intel locks non-K chips and requires specific motherboards, every NVIDIA and AMD GPU supports overclocking out of the box. A $250 <AffiliateLink name="NVIDIA RTX 5060" /> overclocks just as easily as a $2,000 <AffiliateLink name="NVIDIA RTX 5090" />.

**Recovery is instant.** If your CPU overclock is unstable, your system might not boot and you'll need to reset the BIOS. If your GPU overclock is unstable, the driver crashes, the screen goes black for two seconds, and Windows recovers automatically. You just lower the clock speed and try again.

**The silicon lottery still applies.** Just like CPUs, every GPU chip is unique. Two <AffiliateLink name="NVIDIA RTX 5070 Ti" /> cards from the same production run might overclock differently — one might hit +200 MHz on the core while another tops out at +150 MHz. This is normal and expected.

## What You Need Before You Start

### Hardware Requirements

- **Adequate cooling:** Your GPU should stay below 80°C under full gaming load at stock settings. If it's already hitting 85°C+, overclocking will just trigger more thermal throttling. Consider [improving your case airflow](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/pc-case-airflow-guide-best-fan-configuration-gaming-2026) or [undervolting](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/how-to-undervolt-gpu-better-fps-lower-temps-guide-2026) first.
- **Quality PSU:** Your power supply needs headroom. An overclocked GPU draws 10–15% more power. If your PSU is already borderline, overclocking will cause instability or shutdowns. Check our [PSU bottleneck guide](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/can-your-psu-bottleneck-gpu-power-supply-wattage-guide-2026) if unsure.

### Software You'll Need

1. **MSI Afterburner** — The industry-standard GPU overclocking tool. Free, works with all NVIDIA and AMD cards regardless of manufacturer. Download from MSI's website.
2. **3DMark** (free demo) or **Unigine Superposition** (free) — For stability testing. You need a consistent, repeatable GPU stress test.

4. **FrameView** or the in-game benchmark — For measuring actual FPS gains.

## Step-by-Step: Overclocking an NVIDIA GPU (RTX 5000/4000 Series)

### Step 1: Establish Your Baseline

Before touching any settings, you need to know where you're starting from.

1. Open HWiNFO64 and start sensor monitoring. Note your GPU's **stock boost clock** (the speed it actually runs at under load, not the advertised base clock), **temperature**, and **power draw**.
2. Run a benchmark — either 3DMark Time Spy or a demanding game's built-in benchmark. Record the score or average FPS.
3. Play your most demanding game for 15 minutes and note the maximum temperature. This is your thermal baseline.

**Key numbers to record:**
- Stock boost clock under load (e.g., 2,600 MHz)
- Max temperature under sustained gaming (e.g., 74°C)
- Benchmark score or average FPS
- Power draw under load (e.g., 220W)

### Step 2: Overclock the Core Clock

Open MSI Afterburner. You'll see several sliders. Here's what matters:

- **Core Clock (MHz):** This is your primary overclock. It adds MHz on top of the GPU's boost algorithm.
- **Memory Clock (MHz):** We'll handle this separately.
- **Power Limit (%):** Increase this first to give the GPU room to boost higher.
- **Fan Speed:** Leave on auto for now. We'll revisit if temperatures are tight.

**The process:**

1. **Raise the power limit to maximum** (usually 105–115%). This doesn't overclock anything — it just gives the GPU permission to draw more power before throttling. There's zero risk here.
2. **Increase core clock by +25 MHz.** Click Apply.
3. **Run a quick stress test** — 3DMark Time Spy or 10 minutes of a demanding game.
4. **Check for artifacts.** Look for flickering pixels, strange colored blocks, screen glitches, or texture corruption. If you see any, your overclock is too high.
5. **If stable, increase by another +25 MHz.** Repeat the test.
6. **Keep going until you crash or see artifacts.** When the driver crashes (screen goes black momentarily), you've found your limit.
7. **Back off by 25–50 MHz from the crash point.** This is your stable core overclock.

**Typical results by GPU:**

| GPU | Stock Boost | Typical Stable OC | Expected FPS Gain |
|-----|------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| <AffiliateLink name="NVIDIA RTX 5090" /> | ~2,900 MHz | +100–175 MHz | 3–6% |
| <AffiliateLink name="NVIDIA RTX 5080" /> | ~2,700 MHz | +125–200 MHz | 5–8% |
| <AffiliateLink name="NVIDIA RTX 5070 Ti" /> | ~2,600 MHz | +150–225 MHz | 5–9% |
| <AffiliateLink name="NVIDIA RTX 5070" /> | ~2,500 MHz | +150–250 MHz | 6–10% |

Higher-end GPUs tend to be closer to their silicon limits from the factory, so the percentage gains are often smaller than on mid-range cards.

### Step 3: Overclock the Memory

Memory overclocking is often overlooked but can deliver significant gains — especially at higher resolutions where memory bandwidth matters more.

1. **Keep your stable core overclock applied.**
2. **Increase memory clock by +50 MHz.** Click Apply.
3. **Run 3DMark Time Spy and compare the score to your baseline.**
4. **Increase by another +50 MHz.** Repeat.
5. **Watch for the score to start decreasing.** GDDR memory doesn't crash cleanly like the core — instead, it activates error correction that slows performance. If your benchmark score drops despite higher clocks, you've gone too far.
6. **Find the sweet spot** — the highest memory clock where your benchmark score is still improving.

**Typical memory overclock ranges:**

- **GDDR6X (RTX 4000 series):** +200 to +500 MHz
- **GDDR7 (RTX 5000 series):** +150 to +400 MHz
- **GDDR6 (RX 9070 series):** +100 to +300 MHz

### Step 4: Final Stability Test

With both core and memory overclocked:

1. Run 3DMark Time Spy **twice** back to back. Scores should be within 1% of each other.
2. Play your most demanding game for at least 30 minutes. Watch for artifacts, crashes, or unusual stuttering.
3. Monitor temperatures throughout — your new max temp should ideally stay below 83°C.
4. If anything is unstable, lower the core clock by 15 MHz or the memory clock by 25 MHz and retest.

### Step 5: Save Your Profile

In MSI Afterburner, save your stable settings to a profile slot (the numbered buttons at the bottom). Enable "Apply overclocking at system startup" in settings so your overclock persists across reboots.

## Step-by-Step: Overclocking an AMD GPU (RX 9000/7000 Series)

AMD GPUs can be overclocked through either MSI Afterburner or AMD's built-in Adrenalin software. Adrenalin offers some AMD-specific features, so we'll cover that approach.

### Using AMD Adrenalin Software

1. Open AMD Adrenalin Software and navigate to **Performance** → **Tuning**.
2. Switch from **Default** to **Custom** tuning.
3. Under **GPU Tuning**, enable **GPU Clock Speed** override.
4. **Increase the Max Frequency slider by 25–50 MHz** at a time.
5. Optionally increase the **Power Limit** slider to give the card more headroom.
6. Under **VRAM Tuning**, enable **Memory Clock** override and increase by 25–50 MHz at a time.
7. Click **Apply Changes** and stress test after each adjustment.

AMD's approach differs from NVIDIA in that you can also adjust the voltage curve directly in Adrenalin, giving finer control without needing a third-party tool.

**Typical results for AMD GPUs:**

| GPU | Stock Boost | Typical Stable OC | Expected FPS Gain |
|-----|------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| <AffiliateLink name="AMD RX 9070 XT" /> | ~2,800 MHz | +75–150 MHz | 4–8% |
| <AffiliateLink name="AMD RX 9070" /> | ~2,600 MHz | +100–175 MHz | 5–9% |

## Combining Overclocking with Undervolting: The Advanced Play

Here's where things get interesting. Most guides treat overclocking and undervolting as opposites — one adds performance and heat, the other reduces heat at the cost of performance. But you can do both simultaneously for the best of both worlds.

The technique is called **curve tuning** (or voltage-frequency curve editing):

1. In MSI Afterburner, press **Ctrl+F** to open the voltage/frequency curve editor.
2. You'll see a graph showing what frequency the GPU targets at each voltage point.
3. Find your GPU's current maximum voltage point (e.g., 1.050V at 2,700 MHz).
4. Flatten all points above a lower voltage (e.g., 0.975V) to your target overclock frequency (e.g., 2,750 MHz).
5. This forces the GPU to hit a higher frequency at a lower voltage — more FPS, less heat, less noise.

This approach is more complex but can deliver 5–10% more FPS while simultaneously dropping temperatures by 5–10°C. ## Common GPU Overclocking Mistakes

### Chasing MHz Instead of Performance

Higher clock numbers don't always mean higher FPS. Some GPUs hit a wall where increasing the core clock causes more power throttling that offsets the frequency gain. Always validate with benchmarks, not clock speed readings.

### Ignoring Memory Overclocking

Many beginners focus entirely on core clocks and skip memory. At high resolutions (1440p and above), memory bandwidth is increasingly important. The memory overclock can deliver half or more of your total FPS gains.

### Not Testing Long Enough

A 5-minute benchmark might seem stable, but long gaming sessions generate more heat and can expose instability. Test for at least 30 minutes of sustained load before calling an overclock stable.

### Overclocking a Thermally Limited Card

If your GPU already runs at 85°C+ at stock, overclocking will just increase power draw and trigger *more* thermal throttling. Fix your cooling situation first — clean the fans, improve case airflow, or repaste the GPU before adding more heat.

## When Not to Overclock

GPU overclocking is low-risk and high-reward for most systems, but there are situations where it doesn't make sense:

- **Your GPU is already thermally throttling.** Overclocking adds heat. If temps are already a problem, [undervolt instead](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/how-to-undervolt-gpu-better-fps-lower-temps-guide-2026).
- **You have a CPU bottleneck.** If your CPU is the limiting factor, a faster GPU won't help. Check with our [bottleneck analyzer](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store) first.
- **Your PSU is undersized.** Overclocking increases power draw by 10–15%. A 500W PSU with an RTX 5080 is already cutting it close — overclocking could cause shutdowns. See our [PSU guide](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/can-your-psu-bottleneck-gpu-power-supply-wattage-guide-2026).
- **You value silence over performance.** Higher clocks mean more heat, which means louder fans. If you built a quiet PC, keep it that way.
