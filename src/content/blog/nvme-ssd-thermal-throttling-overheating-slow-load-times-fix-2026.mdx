# NVMe SSD Thermal Throttling: Why Your Fast Drive Slows Down Under Load and How to Fix It

You bought a Gen 4 or Gen 5 NVMe SSD rated at 7,000 MB/s. You plugged it in, ran CrystalDiskMark, and the first benchmark looked great. But then you tried copying a 100 GB game install, or you loaded into a massive open-world map — and the speed cratered. Transfer rates dropped to 1,500 MB/s or lower. Game asset streaming stuttered. The drive felt no faster than the budget SATA SSD you replaced.

The most likely cause? **Thermal throttling.** Your NVMe SSD hit its temperature limit and automatically reduced its own speed to prevent hardware damage. This is one of the most overlooked storage bottlenecks in modern gaming PCs — and one of the easiest to fix.

---

## How NVMe Thermal Throttling Works

Every NVMe SSD has a NAND flash memory controller that generates heat during read and write operations. The faster the drive, the more heat it produces. When the controller's temperature exceeds its thermal limit (usually between 70°C and 85°C depending on the manufacturer), the drive's firmware reduces performance to bring temperatures back down.

This is similar to how CPUs and GPUs thermal throttle, but with one critical difference: **most NVMe SSDs ship with no cooling whatsoever.** A bare M.2 drive is a tiny circuit board with no fan, no heatsink, and no airflow path of its own. It relies entirely on whatever cooling your motherboard or case provides — and in many setups, that's almost nothing.

### Why Modern NVMe Drives Run Hotter Than Ever

The thermal problem has gotten worse with each NVMe generation:

- **Gen 3 drives** (3,500 MB/s): Typically stay under 70°C with no heatsink. Rarely throttle in normal use.
- **Gen 4 drives** (7,000 MB/s): Regularly hit 75–85°C under sustained load without a heatsink. Throttling is common during large file transfers and game installs.
- **Gen 5 drives** (12,000+ MB/s): Can exceed 90°C within minutes of sustained writes. Almost always require active cooling or a substantial heatsink to maintain rated speeds.

The jump from Gen 4 to Gen 5 roughly doubled the controller's power consumption. Drives like the Crucial T705 and Samsung 990 EVO Plus Gen 5 can draw 10–12W under peak load — that's as much heat as a small laptop processor concentrated on a chip the size of a postage stamp.

## Signs Your NVMe SSD Is Thermal Throttling

Thermal throttling doesn't announce itself with an error message. It silently degrades performance. Here's how to spot it:

### 1. Game Install and Copy Speeds That Start Fast and Then Collapse

The classic symptom. You start installing a 150 GB game from Steam or copying files from another drive. The first 20–30 GB transfer at full speed (5,000–7,000 MB/s for Gen 4). Then speed drops to 1,000–2,000 MB/s and stays there. The drive hit its thermal limit and throttled.

### 2. Open-World Games With Intermittent Hitching

Games that stream assets from storage in real-time — like *Starfield*, *Cyberpunk 2077*, *GTA VI*, or any large Unreal Engine 5 title — can expose SSD throttling during gameplay. If your drive overheats during an extended session, you'll see micro-stutters as the game's asset streaming pipeline can't pull textures and geometry fast enough. This looks like a GPU or CPU stutter but the root cause is storage.

### 3. Benchmark Scores That Don't Match Rated Specs

Run CrystalDiskMark or AS SSD Benchmark twice in a row without cooling down between runs. If the second run's sequential read/write numbers are significantly lower (30%+ drop), thermal throttling is likely the cause.

### 4. Drive Temperature Above 70°C Under Load

Open **CrystalDiskInfo**, **HWiNFO64**, or **HWMonitor** while running a large file transfer or game install. Check the "Current Temperature" or "Composite Temperature" reading for your NVMe drive. Here's what the numbers mean:

| Temperature | Status | Action Needed |
|---|---|---|
| **Under 50°C** | Cool — excellent | None |
| **50–65°C** | Warm — normal under load | None, but monitor during sustained writes |
| **65–75°C** | Hot — approaching throttle zone | Add a heatsink if you don't have one |
| **75–85°C** | Throttling likely active | Heatsink + airflow improvement needed |
| **Above 85°C** | Heavy throttling, risk of drive degradation | Immediate cooling intervention required |

Most NVMe controllers begin throttling between 70°C and 80°C. The exact threshold varies by manufacturer — Samsung drives tend to throttle at 70°C, while Western Digital and Crucial controllers often tolerate up to 80–83°C before reducing speed.

## Why Your Motherboard's M.2 Slot Location Matters

Not all M.2 slots on your motherboard are equal when it comes to thermals. Where your SSD sits determines how hot it gets:

### The GPU Sandwich Problem

Many motherboards place the primary M.2 slot directly underneath the graphics card. Your GPU's backplate radiates heat downward onto the SSD, which is also receiving heat from the chipset below. The SSD is effectively sandwiched between two heat sources with no airflow.

If your GPU has a large heatsink or runs hot (like the RTX 5080 or RX 9070 XT), the M.2 slot directly beneath it can be 10–15°C hotter than a slot further from the GPU. That difference alone can push a Gen 4 drive from "warm but fine" into throttling territory.

### Slot Priority

Most motherboards with two or more M.2 slots label them M.2_1 and M.2_2 (or similar). If one slot sits under the GPU and another is above or between slots, **put your primary gaming drive in the slot with better airflow** — even if the under-GPU slot connects directly to the CPU lanes. A Gen 4 drive running at full speed through the chipset is faster than a Gen 4 drive throttling at half speed through a direct CPU connection.

Check your motherboard manual for which slots connect to the CPU versus the chipset, and weigh that against the thermal environment of each slot.

## How to Fix NVMe Thermal Throttling

There are several solutions, ranked from easiest and cheapest to most effective:

### 1. Use Your Motherboard's Built-In M.2 Heatsink

Most mid-range and high-end motherboards from 2024 onward include M.2 heatsinks — metal plates that sit on top of the SSD with a thermal pad between them. If you built your PC and left this heatsink in the box or never peeled the protective film off the thermal pad, your drive is running bare.

**Expected improvement:** 10–20°C reduction. This alone fixes most Gen 4 throttling issues.

How to install it:
1. Power down your PC and unplug it.
2. Remove the M.2 heatsink (usually one or two small screws).
3. If there's a plastic film on the thermal pad, peel it off.
4. Seat the NVMe drive in the slot and screw it down.
5. Place the heatsink on top, ensuring the thermal pad contacts the drive's controller and NAND chips.
6. Screw the heatsink back into place.

### 2. Add an Aftermarket M.2 Heatsink

If your motherboard didn't include one, or if the included heatsink isn't sufficient (common with Gen 5 drives), aftermarket heatsinks are inexpensive and effective. Good options include:

- **Basic aluminum heatsink with thermal pad** ($5–8) — Passive cooling, no moving parts. Reduces temps by 15–25°C. Sufficient for most Gen 4 drives.
- **Heatsink with integrated mini-fan** ($15–25) — Active cooling for Gen 5 drives or extreme workloads. Reduces temps by 25–35°C. Brands like Thermalright and be quiet! make slim models that fit under most GPUs.
- **Full-cover heatsink with heat pipes** ($20–35) — The nuclear option. Wraps around the entire drive and conducts heat to a larger fin stack. Overkill for most gaming use cases but necessary for sustained Gen 5 write workloads.

When shopping, measure the clearance between your M.2 slot and your GPU (or the next PCIe slot). Many aftermarket heatsinks are taller than motherboard-included ones and may not fit under a thick graphics card.

### 3. Improve Case Airflow Over the M.2 Area

A heatsink without airflow is a heat soak — it absorbs heat but has nowhere to dump it. Make sure your case has actual air moving over the M.2 slot area:

- **Front-to-back airflow pattern:** Front intake fans pull cool air in, rear and top exhaust fans push hot air out. The air passes over the motherboard (and your M.2 slots) on the way through.
- **Bottom intake fans (if your case supports them):** These blow cool air directly upward across the motherboard — highly effective for M.2 slots sandwiched under GPUs.
- **Avoid negative pressure setups:** More exhaust than intake creates a vacuum that pulls unfiltered air through every crack. Aim for slightly positive pressure (more intake CFM than exhaust).

If your case has no direct airflow over the M.2 area, even a small 40mm or 60mm fan zip-tied to a nearby surface and aimed at the SSD can drop temperatures by 10°C.

### 4. Swap to a Better M.2 Slot

If your motherboard has multiple M.2 slots and your primary drive is in the hottest one (usually the slot closest to the GPU), move it to a cooler slot. Check your manual first — some secondary slots run through the chipset and may be limited to Gen 3 speeds. But a Gen 4 drive running at Gen 3 speeds in a cool slot is still faster than a Gen 4 drive throttling in a hot slot.

### 5. Adjust the SSD's Thermal Limit in Firmware (Advanced)

Some NVMe drives allow you to adjust the thermal throttle threshold using manufacturer tools:

- **Samsung Magician** — Lets you view temperature and health for Samsung drives. Some models allow thermal limit adjustment.
- **Western Digital Dashboard** — Similar monitoring for WD/SanDisk drives.
- **Crucial Storage Executive** — For Crucial/Micron NVMe drives.

**Warning:** Raising the thermal throttle point means the drive will run hotter before slowing down. This shortens the drive's lifespan if temperatures remain high. Only raise it if you've already improved cooling and the drive is throttling at a conservative threshold (like 70°C when the NAND is rated for 85°C).

## Gen 5 NVMe: Do You Even Need It for Gaming?

Here's an uncomfortable truth: **Gen 5 NVMe SSDs offer almost zero benefit for gaming over Gen 4 drives.** Game load times are limited by decompression speed (CPU-bound), not raw sequential read throughput. A Gen 4 drive reading at 5,000 MB/s and a Gen 5 drive reading at 12,000 MB/s load the same game level within 1–2 seconds of each other.

Where Gen 5 matters:
- Video editing with 8K RAW footage
- Database workloads with massive random I/O
- Sustained multi-hundred-GB file transfers
- Professional workloads that actually saturate storage bandwidth

Where Gen 5 doesn't matter:
- Game load times
- Game asset streaming
- Windows boot times
- General desktop responsiveness

If you're building a gaming-focused PC and debating between a $120 Gen 4 2TB drive and a $250 Gen 5 2TB drive, the Gen 4 drive is the smarter buy. It runs cooler, costs less, and performs identically in every gaming scenario. Spend the $130 difference on a better GPU — that's a bottleneck that actually affects your frame rate.

## How DirectStorage Changes the Equation

Microsoft's DirectStorage API (and its GPU decompression feature) is slowly rolling out across more PC games. DirectStorage allows game assets to stream directly from your NVMe SSD to GPU memory, bypassing the CPU decompression bottleneck.

For games that fully implement DirectStorage with GPU decompression, NVMe speed starts to matter more — textures and geometry load faster because the GPU's parallel processing handles decompression far more efficiently than the CPU. In these titles, having a throttling NVMe can actually cause visible texture pop-in and LOD transitions.

As of late 2026, only a handful of games fully utilize DirectStorage with GPU decompression, but the list is growing. If you play titles that support it, keeping your NVMe SSD cool enough to sustain peak read speeds becomes more important.

## The Quick Fix Checklist

If you suspect your NVMe SSD is thermal throttling:

1. **Check your temperature** — Open HWiNFO64 or CrystalDiskInfo and watch the drive temp during a large file transfer. If it exceeds 75°C, you're likely throttling.
2. **Install your motherboard's M.2 heatsink** — If it came with one and it's not installed, this is the single biggest improvement.
3. **Verify the thermal pad** — Make sure the protective film is removed and the pad makes full contact with the drive's chips.
4. **Check your M.2 slot location** — If the drive is sandwiched under your GPU, consider moving it to a cooler slot.
5. **Improve case airflow** — Make sure air is actually moving across the motherboard surface, not just through the CPU and GPU coolers.
6. **Consider a Gen 4 drive instead of Gen 5** — For gaming, Gen 4 offers identical load times with dramatically lower heat output.
