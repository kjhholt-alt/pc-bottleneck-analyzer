## Why Thermal Paste Matters More Than You Think

Thermal paste fills the microscopic gaps between your CPU (or GPU) die and the heatsink base. Without it, air pockets form in those gaps — and air is a terrible thermal conductor. Even a tiny air pocket can create a hotspot that forces your processor to throttle.

Here is why it matters for gaming specifically:

**Thermal throttling directly kills FPS.** When a CPU hits its thermal limit (typically 95-100°C for modern chips), it reduces clock speeds to shed heat. A Ryzen 9800X3D running at 5.2 GHz might drop to 4.0 GHz under thermal throttling — that is a 20%+ drop in single-thread performance, which translates directly to lost frames.

**GPU thermal throttling is even more common.** Graphics cards ship with factory-applied thermal paste that varies wildly in quality. Budget and mid-range GPUs often run 5-10°C hotter than necessary because manufacturers save a few cents on thermal compound. A GPU repaste with quality thermal paste can drop temperatures by 10-15°C on cards that have been running for a year or more.

**Old thermal paste dries out.** Thermal compound degrades over time — it dries, cracks, and loses conductivity. If your PC is 2-3 years old and running hotter than when it was new, a $10 repaste can restore original temperatures without any hardware upgrades.

If your system is showing signs of [thermal throttling](/blog/thermal-throttling-how-heat-kills-pc-performance-and-how-to-fix-it), fresh thermal paste should be the first thing you try before spending money on a new cooler.

## Best Liquid Metal Thermal Compound

Liquid metal compounds use gallium-based alloys instead of silicone-based pastes. They deliver the absolute best thermal transfer — 5-10°C better than the best traditional paste — but they come with serious caveats.

### Thermal Grizzly Conductonaut — Best Liquid Metal

**1g syringe** · ~$15 · Thermal conductivity: 73.0 W/mK

Conductonaut is the gold standard for liquid metal application, and no other compound comes close to its thermal conductivity rating. In our testing, it delivered a staggering 12°C improvement over stock AMD paste — 5°C better than even the Kryonaut Extreme.

**CPU test results (Ryzen 7 9800X3D, PBO2):**
- Peak temp: 75°C
- Average temp: 72°C
- Delta vs stock AMD paste: -14°C

Those numbers are real, but the warnings are equally real:

- **Liquid metal conducts electricity.** A single drop on the PCB, capacitors, or SMDs can permanently kill your CPU or motherboard. You must apply it carefully with the included brush applicator and protect surrounding areas with liquid electrical tape or clear nail polish.
- **It reacts with aluminum.** If your cooler has an aluminum base or cold plate, liquid metal will corrode and destroy it within weeks. Only use it with copper or nickel-plated copper heatsinks. Check your cooler's specifications before applying.
- **It bonds over time.** After several months, liquid metal creates a stain on both the CPU IHS and the cooler base. This is cosmetic only and does not affect performance, but it means you cannot simply wipe it off with a paper towel — you need fine-grit sandpaper or a specialized cleaner.
- **Not suitable for direct-die applications on bare silicon without experience.** GPU dies and delidded CPUs can benefit enormously, but the risk of spillage is higher.

We only recommend liquid metal for experienced builders who understand the risks, need every last degree of thermal headroom, and are comfortable with careful application. For everyone else, the Kryonaut Extreme or NT-H2 gets you 80-90% of the benefit with none of the risk.

## Best Thermal Paste for GPU Repasting

GPU repasting is one of the most underrated performance upgrades you can do. If your graphics card is more than a year old, or if it has always run hotter than reviews suggested, a thermal paste replacement can drop temperatures by 5-15°C. Lower GPU temperatures mean higher sustained boost clocks, which means more FPS.

### Why GPU Repasting Deserves Its Own Category

GPU thermal paste application differs from CPU application in several ways:

- **The die is larger.** A GPU die can be 400-600 mm², compared to a CPU IHS of roughly 1,200 mm² (but the IHS is a heat spreader — the actual die underneath is smaller). You need more paste and better coverage.
- **There are often thermal pads nearby.** VRAM and VRM components use thermal pads that may need replacement when you are already in there.
- **The stock paste is often terrible.** Manufacturers optimize for cost and assembly speed, not thermal performance. Factory application is inconsistent.

**Our GPU repaste pick: Arctic MX-6.** Its slightly thinner viscosity spreads perfectly across GPU dies, it is non-conductive (safe around VRAM and VRM components), and the 4g tube gives you enough for multiple applications. At $7, it is cheaper than a single degree of temperature improvement from any other upgrade.

## Application Methods: Which One Actually Works Best?

The internet has been arguing about thermal paste application methods for over a decade. Here is what our testing shows:

### Center Dot (Pea Method) — Best for CPUs
Place a pea-sized dot in the center of the CPU IHS. Mounting pressure from the cooler spreads it evenly. This method consistently delivered the best and most repeatable results in our testing. Do not spread it manually — let the cooler do the work.

### X Pattern — Best for Large GPU Dies
For GPU repasting, draw a thin X pattern across the die. GPU dies are larger and mounting pressure is often less uniform than CPU coolers, so the X pattern ensures better coverage to the corners.

### Pre-spread — Only for Liquid Metal
Liquid metal must be pre-spread with the applicator brush into a thin, even layer. It does not spread under pressure the way traditional paste does.

### How Much Is Too Much?

More is not better, but slightly too much is far better than too little. Excess paste squeezes out the sides and does no harm (assuming non-conductive paste). Too little paste leaves air gaps that create hotspots. When in doubt, use a slightly larger dot than you think you need.

## When Should You Replace Thermal Paste?

Not every system needs a repaste, but watch for these signs:

- **Temperatures have climbed 5-10°C over the past year** under the same workloads. This is the clearest sign of paste degradation.
- **You are hitting thermal throttle temperatures** (95°C+ on CPU, 90°C+ on GPU hotspot) despite having adequate cooling. Use our [PC performance monitoring guide](/blog/how-to-monitor-pc-performance-while-gaming-best-overlay-guide-2026) to check.
- **Your system is 2-3+ years old** and has never been repasted. Even if temperatures seem fine, a fresh application usually helps.
- **You are installing a new CPU cooler.** Always apply fresh thermal paste when mounting a new cooler — never reuse old paste.
- **You bought a used GPU.** You have no idea what paste was used or how old it is. A repaste should be standard procedure for any secondhand graphics card, along with a [thorough inspection](/blog/how-to-buy-used-gpu-test-mining-damage-hidden-defects-2026).
