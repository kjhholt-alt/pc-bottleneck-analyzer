# How to Tell If Your CPU Is Dying — 7 Warning Signs

Your PC freezes mid-game. No blue screen, no error message — everything just locks up and you have to hold the power button. After a reboot, it works fine for an hour, then freezes again. A week later, you start getting random blue screens with different error codes every time. Is your CPU dying?

Maybe. Or maybe it's a RAM issue, an unstable overclock, a corrupted Windows installation, or even a failing power supply. CPU failure symptoms overlap heavily with other hardware and software problems, and **replacing a CPU that just needed a BIOS update or a RAM reseat is a costly mistake** — especially when it means a new motherboard too.

This guide covers the 7 warning signs of CPU failure, how to distinguish a dying processor from fixable problems, the diagnostic steps to confirm what's actually wrong, and what to do when your CPU really is on its way out.

## Why CPUs Fail

CPUs are among the most reliable components in your PC. While GPUs and power supplies have dozens of potential failure points — fans, VRMs, capacitors, VRAM chips — a CPU is a single monolithic die with no moving parts. Most processors will outlast every other component in your system.

But they're not immortal. Here's what goes wrong:

- **Electromigration** — Over years of use, electrons physically displace metal atoms in the CPU's interconnects, gradually widening some pathways and narrowing others. Eventually, a critical pathway fails. Higher voltages and temperatures accelerate this process significantly.
- **Transistor degradation (NBTI/HCI)** — The billions of transistors in a modern CPU gradually shift their electrical characteristics with use. Threshold voltages drift, switching becomes unreliable, and individual transistors start producing incorrect results. This is the most common cause of age-related CPU failure.
- **Thermal damage** — While modern CPUs have robust thermal protection that throttles and shuts down before damage occurs, years of running at high temperatures (especially with inadequate cooling) accelerate all forms of degradation. CPUs that were heavily overvolted are particularly at risk.
- **Physical damage** — Bent pins (AMD AM4/AM5), damaged LGA pads (Intel), cracked dies from excessive mounting pressure, or static discharge during installation can cause immediate or latent failures.
- **Oxidation and corrosion** — In humid environments or systems exposed to liquid (even microscopic amounts from condensation), the CPU socket contacts can develop corrosion that causes intermittent connection failures.

The average lifespan of a CPU under normal use is **8–15 years**. Heavily overclocked CPUs pushed beyond safe voltages may show degradation within 3–5 years. Intel's 13th and 14th Gen processors have a well-documented degradation issue tied to aggressive power delivery defaults, which Intel addressed with microcode updates — if you're running a 13th or 14th Gen chip, make sure your BIOS is updated.

## The 7 Warning Signs

### 1. Random Freezes That Get Worse Over Time

**What it looks like:** Your system completely locks up — the screen freezes, audio loops or cuts out, and no input responds. You have to hard-reset. At first it happens once a week during heavy loads. Then it starts happening during lighter tasks. Eventually, even web browsing triggers a freeze.

**Why it happens:** When a CPU core produces an incorrect calculation result, the operating system can't recover gracefully. Unlike a GPU crash (where the driver can reset the hardware), a CPU error often means the entire system hangs because the CPU is responsible for managing every other component. As degradation worsens, errors occur at lower and lower workloads.

**Is it definitely the CPU?** Not necessarily. RAM issues are the most common cause of random freezes, followed by power supply problems and driver issues. The distinguishing pattern for CPU failure is **escalation over weeks or months** — problems that steadily get worse with the same hardware and software configuration. RAM issues tend to be more consistent from the start.

### 2. Blue Screens With Different Error Codes

**What it looks like:** You get blue screens (BSODs), but the error code changes each time. One crash says `WHEA_UNCORRECTABLE_ERROR`, the next says `KERNEL_DATA_INPAGE_ERROR`, then `IRQL_NOT_LESS_OR_EQUAL`, then `SYSTEM_SERVICE_EXCEPTION`. There's no pattern to which code appears.

**Why it happens:** A degrading CPU produces random computation errors that corrupt different parts of the operating system depending on which operation went wrong. Because the errors are essentially random, they manifest as different crash types. This is in contrast to a software bug, which typically causes the same BSOD code repeatedly because the same faulty code path triggers the same failure.

**The critical codes to watch for:**
- **WHEA_UNCORRECTABLE_ERROR (0x124)** — This is the strongest single indicator of CPU hardware failure. WHEA (Windows Hardware Error Architecture) directly reports hardware errors detected by the CPU itself. If you see this code, especially repeatedly, your CPU is almost certainly failing.
- **MACHINE_CHECK_EXCEPTION (0x9C)** — Another hardware-level error that the CPU reports when it detects an internal fault. Very strongly correlated with CPU degradation.
- **CLOCK_WATCHDOG_TIMEOUT (0x101)** — Indicates a CPU core failed to respond within the expected time. Can point to a core that's becoming unreliable.

**Rule out first:** A single BSOD, even a WHEA error, doesn't confirm CPU failure. Driver issues, RAM problems, and even a failing SSD can cause BSODs. The key is the pattern — multiple different codes that persist across driver updates and OS reinstalls.

### 3. Boot Failures or POST Problems

**What it looks like:** Your PC occasionally fails to boot. It powers on — fans spin, lights come on — but nothing appears on screen. Or it gets stuck at the BIOS splash screen. Sometimes a reboot fixes it. Sometimes it takes 3–4 attempts to get into Windows. Over time, the successful-boot ratio gets worse.

**Why it happens:** The CPU runs the POST (Power-On Self-Test) sequence that initializes your hardware during boot. If the CPU has degraded cores, the POST process may fail when it attempts to initialize using a faulty core. Because modern CPUs dynamically assign cores, the failing core isn't always the one used during boot — which is why the problem is intermittent.

**Is it the CPU?** Boot failures have many causes — bad RAM is the number one culprit, followed by motherboard issues, PSU problems, and GPU failures. If your motherboard has debug LEDs or a POST code display, it can narrow down the component. A CPU-related POST failure typically shows the "CPU" debug LED lit, or a POST code in the 00–0F range (varies by manufacturer — check your motherboard manual).

### 4. Computational Errors in Stress Tests

**What it looks like:** You run Prime95 (Blend or Small FFTs test) and it reports errors within minutes. The errors might say "FATAL ERROR: Rounding was 0.5, expected less than 0.4" or "Hardware failure detected." OCCT reports errors or stops with a "your CPU is producing incorrect results" message.

**Why it happens:** Stress tests push every part of the CPU to maximum load and verify that computations are correct. A healthy CPU will pass Prime95 for hours without a single error. Even one error in a short run is a strong signal that something is wrong — either the CPU is degrading, or it's being fed unstable power (bad motherboard VRM, unstable overclock, or insufficient voltage).

**This is the most definitive test.** If your CPU fails Prime95 or OCCT at stock settings with default BIOS, after clearing CMOS and testing with known-good RAM, the CPU is almost certainly defective or degrading. No software issue causes mathematical computation errors.

**How to test:**
1. Reset BIOS to defaults (clear CMOS)
2. Run MemTest86 overnight to rule out RAM
3. If RAM passes, run Prime95 Small FFTs for 1 hour
4. Any error = CPU problem (at stock settings with verified-good RAM)

### 5. Single-Core Performance Degradation

**What it looks like:** You run a CPU benchmark like Cinebench and your single-core score has dropped 10–20% compared to when the CPU was new, or compared to published benchmarks for your chip. Multi-core might look fine, or one specific core scores significantly lower than the others.

**Why it happens:** As individual cores degrade, they can no longer sustain their rated boost clocks, or they require more voltage to reach the same frequency. The CPU's internal monitoring detects this and reduces the boost clock for that core. If your i7 used to boost to 5.4 GHz on its best core but now only reaches 5.0 GHz, and no thermal or power limit is the cause, the silicon itself has degraded.

**How to check:** Use HWiNFO64 to monitor per-core clock speeds and VID (voltage) during a single-threaded benchmark. Compare against your chip's specifications. A core that consistently clocks lower than others, or that shows elevated voltage at the same frequency, is showing degradation.

**Rule out first:** Thermal throttling is the most common cause of clock speed drops — check your CPU temperatures first. Also verify your motherboard's power limits haven't been changed and that you're running the latest BIOS. For Intel 13th/14th Gen specifically, the eTVB and TVB boost algorithms are known to be aggressive, and the microcode updates that address degradation intentionally lower boost clocks as a protective measure.

### 6. System Instability After BIOS Update or Fresh OS Install

**What it looks like:** You do a completely clean Windows install on a fresh SSD. No old drivers, no background software, no accumulated junk. And the system is still unstable — crashes, freezes, or blue screens within days. Or you update your BIOS, which resets all settings to defaults, and the instability continues.

**Why it happens:** A fresh OS install eliminates software as a variable. If your system is unstable with a clean Windows install, fresh drivers, and default BIOS settings, only three things can be responsible: the CPU, the RAM, or the motherboard. If you've already tested RAM with MemTest86, the CPU and motherboard are what's left.

**Why this is significant:** Most PC instability comes from software — driver conflicts, Windows corruption, malware, or misbehaving background applications. When you've eliminated all of that and the problem persists, you've narrowed it down to hardware. Combined with other signs on this list (especially WHEA errors or stress test failures), this strongly points to CPU failure.

### 7. Unexpected Shutdowns Under Load

**What it looks like:** Your PC abruptly shuts off — no blue screen, no freeze, just instant power-off as if someone pulled the plug. This typically happens during heavy loads like gaming, rendering, or stress testing. The system might restart on its own or require a manual power-on.

**Why it happens:** Modern CPUs have a built-in thermal shutdown mechanism (THERMTRIP) that instantly cuts power if the temperature exceeds a critical threshold (typically 110–115°C for Intel, 105–115°C for AMD). But instant shutdowns also occur when the CPU detects an unrecoverable internal error — a machine check exception so severe that the system can't even display a blue screen.

**Is it the CPU?** Instant shutdowns are more commonly caused by PSU issues (power supply can't handle the load) or overheating (cooler not seated properly, thermal paste dried out). Check temperatures first with HWiNFO64 — if your CPU is below 85°C when it shuts down, overheating isn't the cause. Check your PSU with our [PSU bottleneck guide](/blog/can-your-psu-bottleneck-gpu-power-supply-wattage-guide-2026). If both are ruled out, the CPU is the likely culprit.

## The Diagnostic Flowchart

Before concluding your CPU is dying, work through this process to rule out other causes:

**Step 1: Check temperatures.** Install [HWiNFO64](https://www.hwinfo.com/) and monitor CPU temperatures during normal use and gaming. If your CPU regularly exceeds 90°C, the symptoms might be thermal throttling, not failure. Fix your cooling first — see our [thermal throttling guide](/blog/thermal-throttling-fix-guide).

**Step 2: Reset BIOS to defaults.** Clear CMOS (check your motherboard manual for the jumper or button). This eliminates any unstable overclock, XMP profile, or aggressive power settings as a cause. If your system is stable at stock BIOS settings, the CPU isn't dying — your previous settings were too aggressive.

**Step 3: Test your RAM.** Download [MemTest86](https://www.memtest86.com/) and boot from the USB. Let it run at least 2 full passes (4+ hours). Even a single error means your RAM is faulty — replace it and retest before blaming the CPU. RAM is responsible for more "CPU dying" false alarms than anything else.

**Step 4: Test with minimal hardware.** Remove all drives except a bootable USB, disconnect all USB devices except keyboard, use only one stick of RAM. If the system is stable with minimal hardware but unstable with everything connected, the problem is a peripheral or drive, not the CPU.

**Step 5: Run a CPU stress test.** With BIOS at defaults and RAM verified, run Prime95 Small FFTs for 1 hour or OCCT CPU test for 30 minutes. Any computation error at this point is a strong indicator of CPU failure.

**Step 6: Check Event Viewer.** Open Windows Event Viewer, go to Windows Logs > System, and filter for WHEA-Logger events (Source: WHEA-Logger). These are hardware errors reported by the CPU itself. Recurring WHEA errors, especially after the other steps above, essentially confirm CPU degradation.

**Step 7: Try a different motherboard (if possible).** This is the nuclear option. If you have access to another compatible motherboard, testing the CPU in a different board rules out motherboard VRM failure or socket damage. If the CPU fails in two different boards, it's the CPU.

## CPU Failure vs. CPU Bottleneck: Know the Difference

A dying CPU and a bottlenecked CPU can look similar on the surface — both result in poor performance during gaming. But they're fundamentally different problems with very different solutions:

| | CPU Failure | CPU Bottleneck |
|---|---|---|
| **Performance** | Erratic — crashes, freezes, errors | Consistent — always limited at same FPS ceiling |
| **Stability** | Gets worse over time | Perfectly stable, just slow |
| **Symptoms** | BSODs, computation errors, POST failures | High CPU usage, low GPU usage, low FPS |
| **Fix** | Replace the CPU | Upgrade to a faster CPU, lower settings, or cap FPS |
| **Detection** | Stress tests fail, WHEA errors in Event Viewer | Monitoring tools show CPU at 100% while GPU is underutilized |

If your system is stable but just slow in games — consistently hitting 100% CPU usage while your GPU idles at 50% — that's a [CPU bottleneck](/blog/cpu-vs-gpu-bottleneck), not a failing CPU. ## How to Extend Your Dying CPU's Life

If you can't replace the CPU immediately, these measures can buy you time:

1. **Undervolt the CPU.** Lowering voltage reduces electromigration and thermal stress. Use your BIOS or Intel XTU / AMD Curve Optimizer to drop voltage while maintaining stability. See our [undervolting guide](/blog/how-to-undervolt-cpu-for-gaming-lower-temps-no-fps-loss-guide-2026).

2. **Lower power limits.** In BIOS, reduce PL1 and PL2 (Intel) or PPT/TDC/EDC (AMD) to reduce the load on degrading transistors. You'll lose some peak performance but gain stability.

3. **Disable failing cores.** If you've identified a specific core that fails stress tests, you can disable it in BIOS (most motherboards have this option). Your CPU becomes an N-1 core processor, but the remaining cores may be perfectly healthy.

4. **Improve cooling.** Better cooling means lower temperatures, which slows all forms of degradation. Even a $30 tower cooler upgrade can drop temperatures 10–15°C. Check our [CPU cooler guide](/blog/best-cpu-cooler-for-gaming-2026-air-aio-ranked-every-budget) for picks.

5. **Update your BIOS.** Motherboard manufacturers release BIOS updates that adjust power delivery and boost algorithms. These updates can reduce stress on a degrading CPU while maintaining reasonable performance.

## Prevention: How to Make Your CPU Last

- **Don't overvolt.** The single biggest factor in CPU longevity. Keep voltage within manufacturer specifications. If you overclock, use the minimum voltage that achieves stability.
- **Maintain good cooling.** Replace thermal paste every 2–3 years. Keep your case well-ventilated. CPU temperatures below 80°C under load are ideal for longevity.
- **Update your BIOS.** Microcode updates can fix known issues with power delivery and boost behavior that accelerate degradation.
- **Use a quality PSU.** Dirty power (voltage ripple) from a cheap power supply stresses every component, including the CPU. A reliable PSU with tight voltage regulation protects your hardware. See our [PSU guide](/blog/best-power-supply-psu-for-gaming-pc-wattage-efficiency-guide-2026).
