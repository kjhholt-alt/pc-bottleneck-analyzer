# Can My PC Run This Game? How to Check System Requirements (2026)

You've been eyeing a new game on Steam, Epic, or Game Pass. The trailer looks incredible. The reviews are glowing. But before you drop $60–70, one question nags: *can my PC actually run this?*

It's a question millions of PC gamers ask every week, and the answer isn't always obvious. Game system requirements are confusing, inconsistent between publishers, and often misleading. "Minimum" doesn't always mean playable. "Recommended" doesn't always mean smooth. And comparing your hardware against a requirements list requires knowing what you actually have — which most people don't memorize.

This guide walks you through the entire process: finding your PC's specs, understanding what system requirements actually mean, matching your hardware against any game's requirements, and knowing what to do when you fall short.

## Step 1: Find Out What Hardware Your PC Has

Before you can compare anything, you need to know what's inside your machine. Here are the fastest ways.

### Method 1: Task Manager (30 seconds)

1. Press **Ctrl + Shift + Esc** to open Task Manager.
2. Click the **Performance** tab.
3. You'll see your **CPU** name at the top of the CPU section, **GPU** name under the GPU section, and total **RAM** at the top of the Memory section.

Write down your CPU model (e.g., "AMD Ryzen 7 7800X3D"), GPU model (e.g., "NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070"), and total RAM (e.g., "32 GB").

### Method 2: DirectX Diagnostic Tool

1. Press **Win + R**, type `dxdiag`, press Enter.
2. The **System** tab shows your CPU, RAM, and Windows version.
3. The **Display** tab shows your GPU name and VRAM.

### The Four Specs That Matter

For game compatibility, you need these four numbers:

| Component | What to look for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **CPU** | Brand, model, generation | Intel Core i7-14700K, AMD Ryzen 7 7800X3D |

## Step 2: Find the Game's System Requirements

Every PC game lists system requirements on its store page. Here's where to find them:

- **Steam:** Scroll to the bottom of any game's store page. Look for the "System Requirements" section.
- **Epic Games Store:** Scroll down on the game page to find system requirements.
- **Xbox/Game Pass:** Check the game's page on the Microsoft Store or Xbox website.
- **Publisher websites:** Many publishers list requirements on the game's official site.

You'll typically see two tiers: **Minimum** and **Recommended**. Some games add a third tier for ultra or 4K settings.

## Step 3: Understanding Minimum vs Recommended Specs

This is where most people get confused. The labels sound straightforward, but their actual meaning varies wildly between publishers. Here's what they *generally* mean in practice:

### Minimum Requirements

**What publishers mean:** The game will launch and be technically playable.

**What you'll actually experience:** Low settings, 1080p, 30 FPS — and sometimes not even that. Some publishers set minimum specs so low that the game runs at 20–25 FPS, which most people would call unplayable. Frame drops in busy scenes are common. Loading times will be long if you're on an HDD.

**Bottom line:** Meeting minimum specs means "the game won't crash on startup." It does NOT mean you'll enjoy playing it.

### Recommended Requirements

**What publishers mean:** A "good" experience at the target resolution.

**What you'll actually experience:** Usually medium-high settings at 1080p and 60 FPS. This is the experience most publishers consider acceptable for the average player. Some publishers target 1440p for their recommended tier, but this varies.

**Bottom line:** Recommended specs are your real target for an enjoyable experience. If you meet these, you can probably play on high settings at 1080p with solid frame rates.

### The Problem With System Requirements

Game requirements have a consistency problem. There's no industry standard for what "minimum" or "recommended" means. One publisher's minimum is 30 FPS at 720p. Another's is 60 FPS at 1080p. A third doesn't specify at all.

Some requirements are also outdated by launch day, written during development when the game was less optimized. Others are wildly conservative because no publisher wants refund requests from players who technically meet minimum specs.

**The most reliable approach:** Treat minimum specs as "the game will launch" and recommended specs as "the game will be enjoyable." If you're between the two, expect to play at lower settings.

## Step 4: How to Compare Your Hardware Against Requirements

### Comparing Your GPU

The GPU is the most important comparison. If your GPU doesn't meet requirements, the game will run poorly regardless of everything else.

The tricky part: GPU naming is confusing. Is an RTX 4060 faster than an RTX 3070? (It depends on the game.) Is an RX 7700 XT better than a GTX 1080 Ti? (Yes, significantly.)

**Quick comparison rules:**

- **Same brand, same generation:** Higher number is faster. RTX 5080 > RTX 5070 > RTX 5060.
- **Same brand, different generation:** It gets complicated. Generally, a new-gen *70-class card matches or beats a previous-gen *80-class card. The RTX 5070 trades blows with the RTX 4080 in many titles.
- **NVIDIA vs AMD:** There's no universal rule. Compare benchmark scores from sources like TechPowerUp or our [hardware database](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store).

If your GPU is one or two tiers below recommended, you can likely still play at medium settings. If it's below minimum, you'll struggle.

### Comparing Your CPU

CPU comparisons are less critical for most games but still matter — especially for CPU-intensive titles like strategy games, city builders, and open-world games with lots of NPCs.

**Quick comparison rules:**

- **Core count:** Most modern games want 6 cores minimum. 8 cores covers you for everything in 2026.
- **Generation matters more than model number.** A modern 6-core CPU (like the Ryzen 5 7600) outperforms older 8-core chips (like the Ryzen 7 2700X) in gaming.
- **Intel vs AMD:** At the same generation and price point, gaming performance is within 5–10%. Focus on generation, not brand.

If you're unsure whether your CPU is fast enough, our [bottleneck analyzer](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store) compares your CPU and GPU against our benchmark database and tells you if your CPU is holding your GPU back.

### Comparing Your RAM

RAM is the simplest comparison:

- **16 GB:** Meets recommended specs for the vast majority of games in 2026.
- **8 GB:** Below minimum for many modern AAA titles. Games will stutter, hitch, or crash. Upgrade priority is high.
- **32 GB:** More than enough for any game. You'll never be RAM-limited.

If a game requires 16 GB and you have 16 GB, you meet the requirement — but you'll want to close background apps (especially Chrome tabs and Discord) to avoid running out. For more on this, see our [guide on how much RAM you need for gaming](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/how-much-ram-do-you-need-for-gaming-2026).

### Comparing Your Storage

Two things matter:

1. **Free space:** Modern AAA games routinely require 80–150 GB. Some (like flight simulators or open-world RPGs) exceed 200 GB. Make sure you have enough free space *plus* 10–15% headroom on your drive.
2. **Drive type:** More games now list SSD as minimum (not just recommended). If you're still on an HDD, expect significantly longer load times and potential texture streaming stutters in open-world games. Our [NVMe bottleneck guide](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/nvme-ssd-bottleneck-is-slow-storage-hurting-gaming-performance-2026) covers why storage speed matters.

## What If Your PC Falls Short?

You checked the specs, did the comparison, and your PC doesn't quite measure up. Don't panic — you have options.

### Option 1: Lower Your In-Game Settings

If you meet minimum but not recommended specs, you can almost always get a playable experience by adjusting settings. The biggest FPS killers to turn down first:

1. **Ray tracing** — Turn it off entirely. It's the single biggest performance hit.
2. **Shadow quality** — Drop from Ultra to Medium. Big FPS gain, barely noticeable difference.
3. **Volumetric effects** — Fog, god rays, and clouds eat GPU power. Medium or Low is fine.
4. **Render resolution** — Keep this at 100% if possible. Dropping it makes everything blurry.
5. **Anti-aliasing** — Switch from TAA or MSAA to FXAA, or use DLSS/FSR if available.

For a complete breakdown, read our [graphics settings guide](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/graphics-settings-explained-which-ones-tank-fps-guide-2026).

### Option 2: Use DLSS, FSR, or Frame Generation

Modern upscaling technology is a game-changer for PCs that fall slightly below requirements. NVIDIA DLSS and AMD FSR render the game at a lower internal resolution and use AI to upscale the image, giving you 30–60% more FPS with minimal visual loss on Quality or Balanced mode.

Frame Generation (DLSS 4 Multi Frame Gen, FSR 4) can effectively double your frame rate on supported hardware. If a game supports these features and your GPU is compatible, you can often jump from "barely minimum" to "above recommended" performance. See our [full DLSS vs FSR comparison](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/dlss-vs-fsr-frame-generation-fix-bottleneck-2026).

### Option 3: Quick Free Optimizations

Before spending money, try these free fixes that can squeeze extra FPS from your existing hardware:

- **Update your GPU drivers.** Outdated drivers can cost you 5–15% performance. See our [GPU driver optimization guide](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/best-nvidia-amd-gpu-driver-settings-maximum-gaming-fps-2026).
- **Close background apps.** Chrome, Discord overlay, and RGB software quietly eat CPU and RAM. Our [background processes guide](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/background-processes-killing-gaming-fps-how-to-fix-2026) shows you what to close.
- **Enable XMP/EXPO.** Your RAM may be running slower than rated. This is a free speed boost most people miss. See our [XMP/EXPO guide](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/xmp-expo-not-enabled-ram-running-slow-how-to-fix-2026).
- **Switch to fullscreen mode.** Borderless windowed adds overhead. [Fullscreen gives you more FPS](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/fullscreen-vs-borderless-windowed-best-display-mode-gaming-fps-2026) in most games.

## Common Scenarios and What They Mean

### "I meet recommended specs but the game runs badly"

This happens more often than it should. Possible causes:

- **Thermal throttling.** Your hardware meets specs on paper, but overheating forces it to slow down. Check our [thermal throttling guide](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/thermal-throttling-how-heat-kills-pc-performance-and-how-to-fix-it).
- **Background processes.** Something is eating your resources. Check Task Manager while gaming.
- **Driver issues.** A bad driver update can tank performance. Try a [clean driver install with DDU](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/clean-install-gpu-drivers-ddu-fix-fps-crashes-stuttering-2026).
- **Single-channel RAM.** If you have one 16 GB stick instead of two 8 GB sticks, you're losing 10–20% performance. See our [single vs dual channel RAM guide](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/single-channel-vs-dual-channel-ram-gaming-bottleneck-2026).
- **The game is just poorly optimized.** Some games run badly on everything. Check if others report similar issues.

### "I'm way below minimum specs"

If your hardware is multiple generations behind minimum requirements, no amount of settings tweaking will make the game playable. At that point, you need to either skip the game or upgrade. Focus your budget on the GPU first — it has the biggest impact on gaming performance. Our [best value graphics cards guide](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store/blog/best-value-graphics-cards-2026) ranks every current GPU by performance per dollar.

### "The game requires an SSD but I only have an HDD"

Some modern games (like Ratchet & Clank: Rift Apart or Star Wars Outlaws) genuinely require SSD-speed storage for their streaming systems. If the game says SSD required (not just recommended), an HDD will cause severe texture pop-in, stuttering, or even crashes. An NVMe SSD is one of the best value upgrades you can make — 1 TB drives start around $60–80.
