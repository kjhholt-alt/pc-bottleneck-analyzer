# Poorly Optimized Game or PC Bottleneck? How to Tell the Difference

You just bought a brand-new game. You have a solid PC — decent CPU, modern GPU, plenty of RAM. You launch the game, and it runs at 40 FPS with stutters. Your first thought: "Is this game just badly optimized, or is something wrong with my PC?"

This is one of the most common questions in PC gaming, and the answer matters. If the game is poorly optimized, you can adjust settings strategically and wait for patches. If your PC is the bottleneck, you know exactly what to upgrade. Getting the diagnosis wrong means wasting time tweaking settings that won't help — or worse, spending money on hardware that wasn't the problem.

This guide shows you how to tell the difference with real diagnostic steps, not guesswork.

---

> **TL;DR**
> - If **all your games** run poorly, it's almost certainly your hardware. If **one specific game** runs poorly while others are fine, the game's optimization is likely the issue.
> - Check GPU and CPU utilization while gaming. A properly optimized game should push one of them close to 100%. If neither is above 60-70%, the game has an engine-level problem.
> - Compare your FPS to benchmarks from reviewers with similar hardware. If they get 90 FPS and you get 40, your PC has a configuration issue. If they also get 40, the game is the bottleneck.

## The Quick Test: Is It One Game or All Games?

Before you open any monitoring tools, answer this question honestly:

**Does the problem happen in every game, or just one?**

- **Every game runs poorly** — Your PC has a bottleneck, a driver issue, or a system-level configuration problem. Games from different studios using different engines don't all have the same optimization issues. If everything is slow, the common factor is your hardware.
- **One specific game runs poorly** — The game's optimization is likely the problem, especially if it's a recent release, a console port, or uses a demanding engine like Unreal Engine 5. That said, some games stress specific components harder than others, so you still need to verify.
- **A few games from the same genre run poorly** — This often points to a specific hardware weakness. Open-world games hammer storage and CPU. Competitive shooters at high refresh rates are CPU-bound. Ray-traced titles need GPU power. The pattern tells you which component to investigate.

This single question eliminates half the diagnostic work. If the answer is "just one game," skip ahead to the game optimization section below.

## How to Diagnose: Hardware Monitoring While Gaming

The most reliable way to separate a bad game from a bad PC is to watch what your hardware is doing in real time. Here's what to look for:

### Step 1: Open a Performance Overlay

Use one of these free tools to display hardware stats while gaming:

- **MSI Afterburner + RTSS** — The gold standard. Shows CPU/GPU usage, temperatures, clock speeds, VRAM, RAM, and per-core CPU utilization.
- **Windows Game Bar** (Win+G) — Basic but built-in. Shows FPS, GPU usage, CPU usage, and RAM.
- **NVIDIA GeForce Experience overlay** or **AMD Adrenalin metrics** — One-click overlays if you use their software.

### Step 2: Read the Numbers

Once you're in-game with your overlay running, look at these three metrics:

**GPU Utilization**
- **90-100%** — Your GPU is the limiting factor. This is normal and expected in GPU-bound scenarios (high resolution, maxed settings). Not a sign of poor optimization unless FPS is much lower than benchmarks suggest.
- **50-80%** — Something is preventing your GPU from working at full capacity. Either your CPU can't feed frames fast enough (CPU bottleneck) or the game engine itself is the problem.
- **Under 50%** — If FPS is also low, the game almost certainly has an optimization issue. A healthy game should be able to fully utilize at least one major component.

**CPU Utilization**
- Check both **overall usage** and **per-core usage**. Many games are single-thread-heavy, so overall CPU usage might show 40% while one core is pinned at 100%. That one maxed core is the bottleneck.
- If no core is above 70% and your GPU is also underutilized, the game engine has a problem — it's not efficiently using your hardware.

**VRAM and RAM Usage**
- If VRAM usage hits your GPU's limit (e.g., 8 GB on an RTX 4060), textures start swapping to system RAM, causing massive stutters. This isn't "bad optimization" — it's a hardware limitation at those settings.
- If system RAM usage is above 90%, you need more RAM or need to close background applications.

### Step 3: The Verdict Matrix

| GPU Usage | CPU Usage (any core) | Likely Cause |
|-----------|---------------------|--------------|
| 95-100% | Under 80% | Normal GPU-bound scenario |
| Under 70% | One core at 95-100% | CPU bottleneck |
| Under 70% | Under 70% (all cores) | Poorly optimized game |
| 95-100% | 95-100% | Balanced system at its limit |
| Fluctuates wildly | Fluctuates wildly | Shader compilation or streaming issue |

The third row is the key one. **When neither your CPU nor GPU is working hard but FPS is low, the game engine is the bottleneck** — it can't generate enough work to keep your hardware busy. This is the hallmark of poor optimization.

## Signs a Game Is Poorly Optimized

Not every performance issue is your hardware's fault. Here are the telltale signs of a badly optimized game:

### 1. Low GPU and CPU Utilization with Low FPS

As described above, this is the clearest signal. A well-made game fills the GPU pipeline efficiently. A poorly optimized one leaves performance on the table because its rendering code, asset streaming, or game logic can't keep up.

### 2. Everyone with Your Hardware Gets Similar Bad Results

Search for benchmarks from hardware reviewers using your exact CPU and GPU. If Digital Foundry, Hardware Unboxed, or GamersNexus also show 45 FPS at your settings, the game is the problem — not your PC. Reviewer systems are clean, properly configured, and using the latest drivers. If they can't get good performance, you won't either.

### 3. Stuttering That Doesn't Correlate with Hardware Load

If the game hitches every few seconds but your CPU, GPU, temperatures, and VRAM are all fine during the stutter, the issue is usually shader compilation (the game compiling graphics shaders on the fly) or poor asset streaming. This is an engine-level problem that no hardware upgrade will fix.

### 4. Performance Gets Worse Over Time in a Session

If the game starts fine at 80 FPS and gradually drops to 40 FPS over an hour of play, it likely has a memory leak — the game allocates RAM or VRAM but never frees it. Watch your memory usage over time. If it climbs steadily without a corresponding increase in what's being displayed, that's a memory leak.

### 5. Specific Areas Tank FPS Regardless of Hardware

If a particular city section, cutscene, or area always drops to 30 FPS, and reports online confirm the same, that area is poorly optimized. It might have excessive draw calls, unoptimized particle effects, or geometry that overwhelms the renderer.

### 6. Day-One Release with Known Issues

Studios frequently ship games with known performance issues that get patched later. Check the game's subreddit, Steam forums, or patch notes. If the developer has acknowledged performance problems or released optimization patches, wait for the fix rather than troubleshooting your PC.

## Signs Your PC Is Actually the Problem

Before blaming the game, rule out these common PC-side issues:

### 1. Outdated GPU Drivers

GPU drivers often include game-specific optimizations that launch alongside major titles. A driver from three months ago might perform 15-20% worse in a new game than the latest version. Always update before benchmarking a new release.

### 2. XMP/EXPO Not Enabled

Over 60% of the systems scanned by our tool are running RAM at JEDEC defaults — far below the rated speed. DDR5-6000 running at 4800 MHz leaves 5-15% FPS on the table, especially in CPU-bound scenarios. Check your BIOS.

### 3. Thermal Throttling

If your CPU or GPU temperatures are above 90-95 degrees Celsius, your hardware is throttling itself to avoid damage. Performance drops from thermal throttling look like bad optimization but are entirely a hardware/cooling issue. Monitor temperatures during gaming.

### 4. Background Processes Eating Resources

Windows updates, antivirus scans, Discord overlay, browser tabs with video playing, recording software — all of these consume CPU, RAM, and sometimes GPU resources. Check Task Manager for anything using more than 5% CPU while gaming.

### 5. Wrong Display Settings

Running a game at your monitor's native resolution through your GPU while accidentally having it render through integrated graphics is more common than you'd think. Make sure the game is using your dedicated GPU (check NVIDIA Control Panel or AMD Adrenalin), and verify you're connected to the right display output.

### 6. Storage Bottleneck Causing Stutters

If your game is installed on a mechanical hard drive, asset streaming stutters are your drive's fault, not the game's. Modern open-world games are designed for SSD speeds. Moving the game to an [NVMe SSD](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=NVMe+SSD+1TB+gaming&tag=bottleneck20-20) often eliminates stuttering entirely.

## What to Do When the Game Is Poorly Optimized

If your diagnosis points to the game, not your PC, here's how to get the best experience:

### Adjust Settings Strategically

Don't just slam everything to Low. Target the settings that cause the biggest FPS hit with the least visual loss:

- **Ray tracing** — Turn it off first. It's the single most demanding feature in modern games and often provides subtle visual improvements in exchange for 30-50% FPS loss.
- **Volumetric fog/clouds** — Extremely demanding. Medium looks nearly identical to Ultra in most games.
- **Shadow quality** — Drop from Ultra to High. The difference is barely visible but often worth 10-15% FPS.
- **Enable DLSS or FSR** — Frame generation and upscaling technologies can recover 40-70% of your lost FPS with minimal visual quality loss. DLSS Quality mode is nearly indistinguishable from native on NVIDIA GPUs; FSR Quality does the same on AMD.
- **Keep texture quality high** — Textures primarily use VRAM, not GPU compute. If you have enough VRAM, maxing textures is essentially free.

### Set a Frame Rate Cap

If the game stutters between 50-80 FPS, cap it at 50 or 60 using RTSS or the in-game limiter. A consistent 60 FPS feels dramatically smoother than an erratic 50-80. V-Sync or a frame rate cap also reduces CPU/GPU load slightly, which can stabilize frame pacing.

### Wait for Patches

Most major releases receive 2-4 optimization patches in the first month. If the game is playable but not great, play something else for two weeks and come back.
