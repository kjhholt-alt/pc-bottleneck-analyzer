## What Are Integrated Graphics, Exactly?

Every modern CPU contains some form of graphics processing hardware built directly into the chip. Unlike a discrete GPU (a separate card with its own dedicated memory and cooling), integrated graphics share the CPU's resources:

- **System RAM** instead of dedicated VRAM — your iGPU borrows from the same memory pool your CPU uses
- **CPU power budget** — the graphics cores share the processor's thermal and power envelope
- **Memory bandwidth** — this is the biggest bottleneck, because system DDR5 bandwidth is a fraction of what dedicated GDDR6/GDDR7 provides

The term **APU** (Accelerated Processing Unit) is AMD's branding for chips where the integrated GPU is powerful enough to be a selling point rather than an afterthought. Not all CPUs with integrated graphics are APUs in the meaningful sense — Intel's UHD 770 has integrated graphics, but nobody is marketing it as a gaming solution.

## The Single Biggest iGPU Performance Factor: Your RAM

Here's something most people don't realize: **your RAM configuration can make or break integrated graphics performance.** Because iGPUs don't have dedicated VRAM, they rely entirely on system memory for both capacity and bandwidth. This makes RAM the single most impactful variable.

### Dual-Channel vs. Single-Channel: Night and Day

Running a single stick of RAM (single-channel) cuts your memory bandwidth in half compared to two sticks (dual-channel). For a CPU, this might cost you 5–10% performance. For an iGPU, it's devastating — expect **30–50% fewer frames** in single-channel mode.

**This is the number one mistake budget builders make.** Buying one 16GB stick instead of two 8GB sticks to "upgrade later" cripples your iGPU from day one.

### RAM Speed Matters More Than Usual

With a dedicated GPU, the difference between DDR5-4800 and DDR5-6000 is usually 2–5% in games. With integrated graphics, that same jump can mean **15–25% more FPS** because you're directly increasing the bandwidth available to the GPU.

**Recommended RAM configurations for iGPU gaming:**

| Configuration | Performance Impact |
|---|---|
| 2x8GB DDR5-6000 CL30 | Optimal — best bang for buck |
| 2x16GB DDR5-6000 CL30 | Optimal with extra capacity |
| 2x8GB DDR5-5200 CL38 | Good — the stock JEDEC spec |
| 1x16GB DDR5-5200 | Terrible — single channel kills iGPU |
| 2x8GB DDR4-3200 | Acceptable on older platforms |

If you're building an APU system, spend the extra $15–20 on faster RAM. It's the highest-impact upgrade per dollar on an integrated graphics system. And make sure [XMP or EXPO is actually enabled in BIOS](/blog/xmp-expo-not-enabled-ram-running-slow-how-to-fix-2026) — your RAM runs at its slowest rated speed by default.

## How to Squeeze Maximum FPS from Integrated Graphics

### 1. Enable FSR or XeSS Upscaling

AMD FSR (FidelityFX Super Resolution) is your best friend on integrated graphics. It renders the game at a lower internal resolution and upscales to your display resolution, dramatically reducing the load on the GPU:

- **FSR Quality** — renders at ~77% resolution, minimal visual loss, 20–30% FPS boost
- **FSR Balanced** — renders at ~67% resolution, slight softness, 35–50% FPS boost
- **FSR Performance** — renders at ~50% resolution, noticeable quality loss, 50–70% FPS boost

Most modern games support FSR, and it doesn't require specific hardware — it works on any GPU, including integrated graphics.

### 2. Optimize In-Game Settings

Not all graphics settings are equal. Some destroy FPS on iGPUs while barely improving visuals. **Turn these down first:**

- **Shadows** — drop from Ultra to Medium/Low (huge FPS gain, minimal visual difference)
- **Volumetric lighting/fog** — extremely GPU-heavy, turn to Low or Off
- **Ambient occlusion** — set to Low or SSAO instead of HBAO+/RTAO
- **Anti-aliasing** — use FSR upscaling instead of TAA/MSAA when possible
- **Draw distance/LOD** — reduce to Medium (saves both GPU and memory bandwidth)
- **Ray tracing** — always OFF on integrated graphics, no exceptions

**Keep these at Medium or higher** (they look good and cost little):

- Texture quality (limited by VRAM allocation, not shader power)
- Anisotropic filtering (virtually free on modern hardware)
- Model detail

For a deeper dive on which settings matter most, see our [Graphics Settings Explained guide](/blog/graphics-settings-explained-which-ones-tank-fps-guide-2026).

### 3. Increase iGPU VRAM Allocation in BIOS

By default, most motherboards allocate only 512MB of system RAM to the iGPU. For gaming, you want more. Enter your BIOS and look for a setting called **UMA Frame Buffer Size**, **iGPU Allocated Memory**, or **VRAM Size**:

- **Recommended: 4GB** for most 1080p gaming
- **Maximum useful: 6–8GB** for games with heavy texture usage
- Don't allocate more than half your total system RAM

This doesn't make the iGPU faster — it just ensures you have enough VRAM to load textures without constant swapping, which causes stuttering.

### 4. Set Your Windows Power Plan

Integrated graphics benefit from maximum CPU clock speeds since the GPU shares the CPU's power budget:

1. Open **Settings > System > Power & battery**
2. Set Power mode to **Best performance**
3. In AMD Adrenalin (or Radeon Settings), set the GPU profile to **Gaming**

On laptops, plug in while gaming — battery power mode significantly throttles both CPU and iGPU clocks.

### 5. Close Background Applications

This matters more on iGPU systems than dedicated GPU systems because you're sharing everything — CPU time, memory bandwidth, and RAM capacity. Chrome with 30 tabs, Discord streaming, and a game running simultaneously will tank your FPS. See our guide on [background processes killing gaming FPS](/blog/background-processes-killing-gaming-fps-how-to-fix-2026) for specifics.

### 6. Consider Resolution Scaling

If a game doesn't support FSR, you can run at a lower resolution:

- **900p (1600x900)** on a 1080p display — 30% fewer pixels, surprisingly decent-looking on a small monitor
- **720p (1280x720)** — significant visual downgrade but doubles FPS compared to 1080p

Use your GPU driver's built-in integer scaling if available for the sharpest low-resolution image.

## When Integrated Graphics Aren't Enough: The Upgrade Decision

Integrated graphics are a spectrum, not a binary. Here's a realistic assessment of when you've hit the iGPU ceiling:

**You're fine on integrated graphics if:**
- You primarily play esports titles (Valorant, CS2, League, Fortnite)
- You're OK with 30–60 FPS at 1080p low-medium settings in older AAA games
- You play strategy, simulation, or indie games that aren't GPU-demanding
- You're building a temporary system and plan to add a GPU later

**You need a dedicated GPU if:**
- You want 60+ FPS in modern AAA games
- You play at 1440p or higher resolution
- You want ray tracing or high-quality visuals
- You play competitive shooters and need 144+ FPS
- You stream while gaming

If you're in the second camp, even a budget dedicated GPU like the <AffiliateLink name="AMD Radeon RX 7600" /> ($230–260) will deliver **3–5x the gaming performance** of the best integrated graphics. It's the single biggest performance jump you can make.

## Common iGPU Gaming Myths, Debunked

### "Integrated graphics can't game at all"
Wrong — at least for AMD APUs. The Radeon 780M is genuinely capable. It's not going to replace a dedicated GPU, but calling it "not for gaming" ignores what it can actually do.

### "You need a dedicated GPU for esports"
No. Valorant at 90–120 FPS on a Radeon 780M is completely viable for ranked play up to most skill levels. You don't need 240 FPS to enjoy competitive gaming.

### "Just buy the cheapest GPU instead of an APU"
This made sense when the cheapest GPU was $100. In 2026, the entry-level dedicated GPU worth buying starts around $200–230. An APU system at $650 with the option to add a GPU later is a legitimate budget strategy.

### "More VRAM allocation = faster iGPU"
Not exactly. Allocating more system RAM to the iGPU gives it more texture memory, but doesn't increase computational speed or memory bandwidth. Set 4GB and forget it unless specific games demand more.
