# Does Anti-Cheat Affect FPS? How Kernel-Level Anti-Cheat Impacts Gaming Performance in 2026

You launch a competitive match. Your hardware is solid. Your drivers are updated. But something feels *off* — random frame time spikes, slightly lower FPS than benchmarks suggested, or a persistent stutter that appears in online matches but vanishes in single-player or practice modes.

The culprit might not be your CPU, GPU, or RAM. It might be the anti-cheat software running invisibly underneath your game.

As cheating has escalated in competitive gaming, developers have responded with increasingly aggressive anti-cheat solutions — many of which now operate at the kernel level of your operating system. That means they have deeper system access than your browser, your game, or even most of Windows itself. And that level of access comes with a performance cost that most gamers never measure.

Let's break down exactly what anti-cheat does to your hardware, which solutions hit hardest, and how to minimize the impact.

## What Anti-Cheat Actually Does Under the Hood

Before you can understand the performance impact, you need to understand what anti-cheat software is actually doing while you play.

At its core, anti-cheat monitors your system for signs of cheating: modified game files, injected code, suspicious memory patterns, known cheat signatures, and abnormal input behavior. To do this, it needs to:

1. **Scan system memory** — Continuously check whether another program has modified the game's memory space (aimbots, wallhacks, speed hacks).
2. **Monitor running processes** — Watch for known cheat tools, debuggers, or injection frameworks that are running alongside the game.
3. **Validate game files** — Verify that game binaries, shaders, and assets haven't been tampered with.
4. **Inspect driver activity** — Check whether any loaded drivers are suspicious or known to facilitate cheating.
5. **Report telemetry** — Send periodic data back to anti-cheat servers about the system state.

Each of these operations costs CPU cycles, memory bandwidth, and sometimes disk I/O. The question is: how much?

## Kernel-Level vs. User-Level Anti-Cheat: What's the Difference?

Not all anti-cheat solutions are created equal. The biggest distinction is *where* they run in your operating system.

### User-Level Anti-Cheat

User-level anti-cheat runs as a regular application — the same privilege level as your game, your browser, or Discord. It can monitor the game's own process and some system state, but it can't see everything happening on the machine.

**Examples:** VAC (Valve Anti-Cheat), older versions of EAC, PunkBuster

**Performance profile:** Generally lightweight. Runs inside the game process or as a simple background service. Typical overhead is 1–2% CPU usage, negligible memory, and minimal impact on frame times.

**Tradeoff:** Easier for cheat developers to bypass because it can't see kernel-level activity.

### Kernel-Level Anti-Cheat

Kernel-level anti-cheat installs a driver that operates at Ring 0 — the same privilege level as your GPU drivers, your storage controller, and the Windows kernel itself. This gives it complete visibility into everything happening on the machine, making it far harder for cheats to hide.

**Examples:** Riot Vanguard (Valorant), Easy Anti-Cheat with kernel driver (Fortnite, Apex Legends, Rust), BattlEye (PUBG, Rainbow Six Siege, Escape from Tarkov, DayZ), Ricochet (Call of Duty), nProtect GameGuard

**Performance profile:** More variable and harder to predict. Because kernel drivers can interact with CPU scheduling, interrupt handling, and memory management, their performance impact depends heavily on your hardware configuration.

**Tradeoff:** More effective at catching cheats, but higher system overhead, potential compatibility issues, and security concerns.

## Real Performance Impact: The Numbers

Let's look at what anti-cheat actually costs you in practice. These numbers reflect aggregate community benchmarking data and controlled testing from hardware reviewers in 2026. Individual results vary depending on hardware and system configuration.

### Average FPS Impact

| Anti-Cheat | Type | Avg FPS Loss | 1% Low Impact | Background CPU Usage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **VAC** | User-level | < 1% | Negligible | 0.1–0.3% |
| **Easy Anti-Cheat** | Kernel driver | 1–3% | 2–5% | 0.5–1.5% |
| **BattlEye** | Kernel driver | 1–3% | 2–6% | 0.5–2.0% |
| **Riot Vanguard** | Kernel (always-on) | 2–4% | 3–7% | 1.0–2.5% (persistent) |
| **Ricochet** | Kernel driver | 1–3% | 2–5% | 0.5–1.5% |
| **nProtect GameGuard** | Kernel driver | 3–8% | 5–12% | 1.5–4.0% |

A few things stand out:

**The 1% low impact is always worse than the average FPS impact.** This is the key insight most gamers miss. Anti-cheat scanning happens in bursts — periodic memory sweeps, process enumerations, integrity checks. These bursts compete with your game's main thread for CPU time, causing frame time spikes that drag down your 1% lows without significantly affecting your average FPS.

**nProtect GameGuard is the heaviest.** Commonly used in Korean MMOs and some global titles, GameGuard has a reputation for aggressive scanning that noticeably impacts performance, especially on lower-end hardware.

**Vanguard's always-on model has a unique cost.** Unlike other anti-cheats that only activate when you launch a game, Vanguard starts at boot and runs continuously. This means it's consuming resources before your game even starts, reducing the CPU headroom available for gaming.

## Why Anti-Cheat Hits Some PCs Harder Than Others

A gaming rig with a <AffiliateLink name="AMD Ryzen 7 9800X3D" /> and 32GB of DDR5 RAM barely notices anti-cheat overhead. But a budget build with a quad-core CPU and 16GB of DDR4 might see real performance degradation. Here's why:

### Core Count Matters Most

Anti-cheat scanning needs CPU time. On a CPU with 8 or more cores, those scans run on cores that the game isn't fully utilizing. On a 4-core/4-thread CPU, the anti-cheat competes directly with the game's main threads.

**Impact by core count:**

| CPU Cores/Threads | Anti-Cheat FPS Impact | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 4C/4T | 5–10% | Significant competition for threads |
| 4C/8T | 3–6% | Hyperthreading helps absorb scanning load |
| 6C/12T | 1–3% | Sweet spot — enough headroom for both |
| 8C/16T+ | < 2% | Anti-cheat has dedicated core headroom |

This is one reason why the jump from a quad-core to a six-core CPU often feels bigger than benchmarks suggest — it's not just the game getting more threads, it's every background process (including anti-cheat) getting out of the game's way.

### RAM Capacity and Speed

Anti-cheat memory scanning is bandwidth-sensitive. On systems with slower RAM (DDR4-2400/2666 or single-channel configurations), anti-cheat memory sweeps take longer and are more likely to cause cache evictions that slow down the game.

Running 32GB of dual-channel DDR5 means there is enough headroom that anti-cheat memory operations don't compete with game data. At 16GB of DDR4, that headroom is tighter.

### Storage Speed

Some anti-cheats perform periodic file integrity checks on game assets. On an NVMe SSD, these checks complete in milliseconds. On a SATA SSD, they take longer. On an HDD, they can cause brief but noticeable hitches, especially during level transitions or when loading new assets.

## How to Minimize Anti-Cheat Performance Impact

You can't disable anti-cheat (not without getting banned), but you can minimize its impact:

### 1. Close Unnecessary Background Programs

Anti-cheat is one of many background processes competing for CPU time. Reducing the total background load gives both your game and the anti-cheat more room to breathe. Close browsers, Discord overlays, RGB software, and cloud sync apps before competitive sessions.

For a complete guide on this, see our article on [background processes killing gaming FPS](/blog/background-processes-killing-gaming-fps-how-to-fix-2026).

### 2. Make Sure Your RAM Is Running at Full Speed

If your RAM is running at JEDEC default speeds (often 2133 MHz or 4800 MT/s) instead of its rated XMP/EXPO speed, you're leaving free performance on the table — and making anti-cheat memory scans take longer than necessary.

Check your BIOS settings. If XMP or EXPO isn't enabled, follow our guide on [fixing RAM running below rated speed](/blog/xmp-expo-not-enabled-ram-running-slow-how-to-fix-2026).

### 3. Avoid Single-Channel RAM

Running a single stick of RAM cuts your memory bandwidth in half. Anti-cheat scanning, which is memory-intensive, will hit harder on single-channel systems. Always run two sticks in dual-channel configuration.

### 4. Keep Your CPU Thermals in Check

When anti-cheat adds 2–4% CPU load on top of your game, it pushes total CPU utilization higher. If your CPU is already running warm, that extra load can push it into thermal throttling territory — turning a 2% overhead into a 10%+ performance cliff.

Make sure your cooling solution keeps your CPU below 85°C under gaming load. See our [thermal throttling guide](/blog/thermal-throttling-how-heat-kills-pc-performance-and-how-to-fix-it) for details.

### 5. Consider Core Affinity (Advanced)

On some systems, you can use Process Lasso or Windows Task Manager to set CPU affinity for anti-cheat processes, pinning them to specific cores that your game doesn't prioritize. This is an advanced technique and doesn't work with all anti-cheat solutions (some will flag affinity manipulation as suspicious), but when it works, it can eliminate frame time spikes.

### 6. Restart Before Competitive Sessions (for Vanguard)

Because Vanguard runs from boot, it accumulates memory overhead over long sessions. If you've had your PC on all day doing productivity work, a fresh restart before a Valorant session ensures Vanguard starts clean and your system has maximum available resources.

## Anti-Cheat and Linux Gaming: A Special Case

If you're gaming on Linux via Steam Proton or Wine, anti-cheat compatibility is still a significant factor in 2026. While Easy Anti-Cheat and BattlEye both have official Linux support through Proton, some games still don't enable it, and kernel-level solutions like Vanguard and Ricochet do not work on Linux at all.

For games where anti-cheat does work on Linux, the performance overhead tends to be similar to Windows, sometimes slightly higher due to the translation layer. The main bottleneck isn't the anti-cheat itself — it's whether the game developer has opted into Proton compatibility for their anti-cheat solution.

## When Anti-Cheat Is Actually Your Bottleneck

How do you tell if anti-cheat is the thing holding back your performance? Look for these signs:

1. **Offline or practice modes run significantly smoother than online matches** in the same game. If your FPS is 15–20% higher in practice mode, anti-cheat overhead (combined with networking) is likely a factor.

2. **Frame time spikes occur at regular intervals.** Anti-cheat scans often run on timers. If you see periodic stutters every 10–30 seconds, that's a telltale pattern of periodic scanning hitting your main thread.

3. **Your CPU usage is unexpectedly high at idle.** Before launching a game, check Task Manager. If anti-cheat services are consuming more than 2% of your CPU at idle, something may be misconfigured.

4. **Your 1% lows are disproportionately worse than your average FPS.** If your average is 144 FPS but your 1% lows are below 80, and you've ruled out thermal throttling and background processes, anti-cheat scanning bursts are a likely contributor.

## The Bottom Line

Anti-cheat software does affect gaming performance, but for most modern gaming PCs (6+ core CPUs, 16+ GB of RAM, NVMe storage), the impact is small — typically 1–3% average FPS and 2–6% on 1% lows. It's noticeable but not game-breaking.

Where anti-cheat becomes a real problem is on budget or older systems with quad-core CPUs, slow RAM, or insufficient cooling. On those machines, the overhead stacks with other background load to create CPU bottlenecks that drag the entire experience down.

The fix isn't removing anti-cheat — it's making sure your hardware has enough headroom to handle both your game and the security layer running underneath it. A six-core CPU with fast dual-channel RAM and solid thermals absorbs anti-cheat overhead without breaking a sweat.
