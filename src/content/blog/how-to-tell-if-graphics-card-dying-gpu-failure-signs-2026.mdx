# How to Tell If Your Graphics Card Is Dying — 8 Warning Signs

Your game freezes. The screen goes black for two seconds, then comes back. A few days later it happens again, but this time you see weird colored pixels scattered across the screen before the crash. Your heart sinks — is your GPU dying?

Maybe. Or maybe it's a driver bug, overheating, a bad cable, or a power delivery issue. The symptoms of a dying graphics card overlap heavily with fixable software and hardware problems, and **replacing a GPU that just needed a driver reinstall is an expensive mistake**.

This guide covers the 8 warning signs of GPU failure, how to distinguish hardware death from software issues, the diagnostic steps to confirm what's actually wrong, and what to do if your card really is on the way out.

## Why GPUs Fail

Modern graphics cards are complex pieces of engineering — billions of transistors, high-speed VRAM chips, VRM circuits pushing hundreds of watts, and thousands of solder joints connecting it all together. Over time, several things can go wrong:

- **Solder joint degradation** — Repeated thermal cycling (heating up during gaming, cooling down at idle) stresses the solder connections between the GPU die and the PCB. Micro-cracks develop over years and eventually break connections.
- **VRAM failure** — Individual memory chips can develop bad sectors, similar to how a hard drive develops bad blocks. This causes artifacts specifically in textures and frame buffers.
- **VRM component failure** — The voltage regulation modules that deliver power to the GPU can degrade, causing unstable power delivery under load. Capacitors dry out, MOSFETs weaken, and the card can't sustain its boost clocks.
- **Fan bearing failure** — Less dramatic than die failure, but a GPU that can't cool itself will throttle and eventually shut down to prevent damage.
- **Physical damage** — A card that's been dropped, exposed to liquid, or run in an environment with excessive dust and humidity will degrade faster.

The average lifespan of a well-maintained graphics card is **5–8 years** of regular gaming use. Cards that were mined on 24/7, ran in poorly ventilated cases, or experienced extreme thermal cycling may fail sooner.

## The 8 Warning Signs

### 1. Visual Artifacts Under Load

**What it looks like:** Random colored pixels, lines, or blocks appear on screen during gaming or GPU-intensive tasks. They might flicker in and out or persist until you restart. Common patterns include:

- Scattered bright pixels (red, green, blue, or white dots)
- Geometric shapes or triangles stretching across the screen
- Texture corruption — game textures turn into garbled noise or solid colors
- Screen-wide color tinting (everything turns pink, green, or has a color cast)

**Why it happens:** Artifacts typically indicate VRAM failure (bad memory cells producing corrupt data) or GPU die degradation (processing units producing incorrect calculations). The reason they appear under load is that gaming uses more of the GPU's resources, exposing failing areas that idle use doesn't touch.

**Is it definitely hardware?** Not always. Driver bugs, unstable overclocks, and overheating can also cause artifacts. The key test: if artifacts appear **after a clean driver reinstall and with no overclock applied**, and they get progressively worse over weeks, that's hardware failure.

### 2. Driver Crashes That Increase in Frequency

**What it looks like:** You see "Display driver has stopped responding and has recovered" or "Display driver nvlddmkm stopped responding" (NVIDIA) errors. The screen goes black for 1–5 seconds, then recovers. Initially it happens once a week, then once a day, then multiple times per session.

**Why it happens:** When the GPU fails to complete a rendering operation within Windows' timeout period (typically 2 seconds), the OS resets the driver. Occasional occurrences are normal — every GPU driver has edge-case bugs. But an **escalating pattern** — increasing frequency with the same driver version, same games, same settings — strongly suggests hardware degradation.

**The critical distinction:** A driver crash that appeared after a driver update and goes away when you roll back is a software bug. A driver crash that persists across multiple driver versions and gets worse over time is likely hardware.

### 3. Black Screen Crashes During Gaming

**What it looks like:** Your display goes completely black during a game. Sometimes the game audio continues for a few seconds before the system freezes entirely. You have to hard-reset (hold the power button). The system might recover on reboot, or it might black-screen again within minutes.

**Why it happens:** This is often a power delivery failure — the VRM can't maintain stable voltage under peak load, the GPU loses power momentarily, and the display signal drops. It can also indicate a dying GPU die that crashes during complex shader operations.

**Rule out first:** An undersized or failing PSU can cause identical symptoms. If your power supply is marginal for your GPU (e.g., a 550W PSU with an RTX 4070 Ti), the PSU may be the culprit, not the GPU. Check our [PSU bottleneck guide](/blog/psu-bottleneck-signs-your-power-supply-is-killing-performance) to rule this out.

### 4. Fan Behavior Changes

**What it looks like:** One or more GPU fans stop spinning entirely, spin at inconsistent speeds, make grinding/clicking/whining noises, or spin at 100% regardless of load. On cards with zero-RPM idle mode, the fans might never spin up even during gaming.

**Why it happens:** Fan bearings wear out over time, especially sleeve bearings (cheaper cards) which degrade faster than ball bearings. Fan controller circuits on the PCB can also fail, sending incorrect speed signals.

**How serious is it?** Fan failure itself won't kill your GPU immediately, but it will cause thermal throttling and dramatically reduced performance. Left unchecked, sustained high temperatures accelerate other forms of degradation. Individual fans can often be replaced for $10–20 if you can find the right model for your card.

### 5. Performance Degradation Over Months

**What it looks like:** A game that used to run at 120 FPS now barely holds 90 FPS with the same settings. Benchmark scores are 15–25% lower than when the card was new. The decline is gradual — not a sudden drop after a driver update, but a slow slide over months.

**Why it happens:** As GPU components degrade, the card may silently reduce its boost clocks to maintain stability. VRAM errors might force error-correction overhead. VRM degradation limits power delivery, capping performance below rated specs.

**Rule out first:** Driver updates, Windows updates, game patches, and background software changes can all affect performance. A clean Windows install with fresh drivers that still shows reduced performance is a stronger indicator of hardware decline. Also check that [thermal throttling](/blog/thermal-throttling-how-heat-kills-pc-performance-and-how-to-fix-it) isn't the cause — degraded thermal paste on an older card causes the same gradual performance loss.

### 6. Artifacts or Corruption in BIOS/POST Screen

**What it looks like:** You see garbled text, missing characters, colored blocks, or distorted graphics on the motherboard splash screen or in BIOS — before Windows even loads.

**Why it happens:** The BIOS/POST screen uses the GPU's basic display output with no driver involved. If you see corruption here, it's almost certainly hardware — no software driver is loaded yet, so driver bugs are ruled out.

**This is the strongest single indicator of GPU hardware failure.** Artifacts during POST mean the GPU silicon or VRAM is failing at a fundamental level. If you see this consistently, back up your data and start shopping for a replacement.

### 7. System Fails to POST with GPU Installed

**What it looks like:** You press the power button, fans spin, but you get no display output at all. The motherboard might show a VGA error code on its debug LEDs. Removing the GPU and using integrated graphics (if your CPU has them) restores normal boot.

**Why it happens:** The GPU has failed to the point where it can't initialize its display pipeline at all. The motherboard detects the GPU on the PCIe bus but can't get a valid response from it.

**Before declaring it dead:** Try reseating the GPU (remove and reinstall it). Try a different PCIe slot if available. Try a different display cable. Try the GPU in a different system entirely to confirm. A GPU that fails in multiple systems is confirmed dead.

### 8. Blue Screens with GPU-Related Stop Codes

**What it looks like:** Windows blue screens (BSOD) with stop codes like `VIDEO_TDR_FAILURE`, `VIDEO_SCHEDULER_INTERNAL_ERROR`, `SYSTEM_THREAD_EXCEPTION_NOT_HANDLED (nvlddmkm.sys)`, or `DPC_WATCHDOG_VIOLATION` during GPU-intensive tasks.

**Why it happens:** These stop codes indicate the GPU driver encountered an unrecoverable error. While driver bugs can cause individual occurrences, **persistent BSODs across multiple driver versions** with these specific codes point to failing hardware that the driver can't work around.

**Important caveat:** `VIDEO_TDR_FAILURE` is one of the most common BSODs and is caused by driver issues far more often than hardware failure. Only suspect hardware if it persists after a [clean driver reinstall using DDU](/blog/clean-install-gpu-drivers-ddu-fix-fps-crashes-stuttering-2026).

## How to Diagnose: Software vs. Hardware Failure

Before buying a replacement, work through this diagnostic sequence:

### Step 1: Clean Driver Reinstall with DDU

Download [DDU (Display Driver Uninstaller)](https://www.wagnardsoft.com/), boot into Safe Mode, and completely remove all GPU driver traces. Then install the latest stable driver (not beta). Check our [DDU clean install guide](/blog/clean-install-gpu-drivers-ddu-fix-fps-crashes-stuttering-2026) for the full walkthrough.

**If the problem disappears after DDU:** It was a driver issue, not hardware failure.

### Step 2: Remove Any Overclocks

If you've overclocked your GPU using MSI Afterburner or similar tools, reset to stock clocks and voltages. An unstable overclock produces identical symptoms to hardware failure — artifacts, crashes, black screens. Also check if the manufacturer's factory overclock is aggressive: some AIB cards ship with OC profiles that can be unstable on less lucky silicon samples.

### Step 3: Check Temperatures Under Load

Run a stress test like FurMark or 3DMark Time Spy and monitor GPU temperatures with HWiNFO64. If the GPU hits its thermal limit (83–87°C for NVIDIA, 90–110°C junction for AMD) and then crashes, the problem might be thermal — not the GPU dying. Repasting the GPU thermal compound can fix this completely.

### Step 4: Run a GPU Stress Test

Use **FurMark** (free) or **3DMark** (free demo) to put the GPU under maximum sustained load:

1. Close all other applications.
2. Run the stress test for at least 15 minutes.
3. Watch for artifacts, crashes, or driver resets during the test.
4. Compare your benchmark score to the average for your card model.

A score significantly below average (15%+ lower) with no thermal throttling suggests hardware degradation. Artifacts or crashes during a stress test at stock clocks after a clean driver install are strong evidence of failing hardware.

### Step 5: Test in a Different System

The definitive test. Install your GPU in a different PC with a known-good PSU and monitor. If the problems follow the GPU, it's the card. If the problems stay with the original PC, suspect the PSU, motherboard PCIe slot, or cables.

## "My GPU Is Dying — What Do I Do?"

If you've worked through the diagnostics and confirmed hardware failure, here's your action plan:

### 1. Check Your Warranty

Most GPU manufacturers offer 3-year warranties. EVGA (discontinued but still honoring warranties), ASUS, MSI, Gigabyte, and Sapphire all cover manufacturing defects. If your card is under warranty, RMA it before spending money on a replacement.

### 2. Back Up Everything

A GPU that's showing signs of failure can die completely at any time. Make sure your important files are backed up. The GPU failing won't destroy your data (that's on your storage drives), but repeated hard crashes from GPU failures can corrupt your Windows installation or game saves.

### 3. Use Integrated Graphics as a Stopgap

If your CPU has integrated graphics (all Intel Core processors except F-series, AMD Ryzen with "G" suffix), you can remove the dying GPU and connect your monitor to the motherboard's display output. You won't be gaming at high settings, but you'll have a functional PC while you wait for a replacement.

## How to Make Your New GPU Last Longer

Once you've replaced the failed card, these practices extend the lifespan of its successor:

- **Maintain good airflow** — A GPU running 10°C cooler has dramatically less thermal stress on its solder joints. Check our [case airflow guide](/blog/pc-case-airflow-guide-best-fan-configuration-gaming-2026).
- **Set a custom fan curve** — Don't let the GPU sit at 85°C for hours. Use MSI Afterburner to set the fans to ramp earlier. A slightly louder card that runs at 72°C will outlast a quiet one running at 84°C.
- **Avoid extreme overclocks on daily use** — Occasional benchmarking is fine, but running max voltage 24/7 accelerates electromigration and component wear.
- **Clean your PC regularly** — Dust insulates heatsinks and blocks airflow. Compressed air every 3–6 months prevents thermal buildup. See our [PC cleaning and maintenance guide](/blog/how-to-clean-gaming-pc-dust-thermal-paste-maintenance-better-fps-2026).
- **Use a quality PSU** — Cheap power supplies deliver dirty, unstable power that stresses GPU VRMs. A good 80+ Gold PSU protects every component in your system. Check our [PSU guide](/blog/best-power-supply-psu-for-gaming-pc-wattage-efficiency-guide-2026).
- **Undervolt for efficiency** — Running your GPU at a lower voltage for the same clock speed reduces heat and power consumption with zero performance loss. See our [undervolting guide](/blog/how-to-undervolt-gpu-better-fps-lower-temps-guide-2026).
