# How to Undervolt Your CPU for Gaming: Lower Temps Without Losing FPS (2026)

Your CPU is running hotter than it needs to. That's not a guess — it's a near-certainty for anyone using a modern AMD Ryzen or Intel Core processor with stock settings. CPU manufacturers ship their chips with voltage curves tuned for worst-case silicon, meaning the average chip receives more voltage than it actually needs to maintain stability. That excess voltage converts directly into waste heat, pushing temperatures higher, fans louder, and in some cases triggering the thermal throttling that quietly steals your gaming performance.

Undervolting fixes this by reducing the voltage your CPU receives while keeping clock speeds identical. The result: 10–25°C lower temperatures, quieter fans, and zero performance loss. In fact, many gamers report *higher* sustained performance after undervolting because their CPU stops thermal throttling during extended sessions.

If you've already optimized your [GPU undervolt](/blog/how-to-undervolt-gpu-better-fps-lower-temps-guide-2026) and you're still seeing high temps, the CPU is your next target. Here's exactly how to do it safely on both AMD and Intel platforms.

> **TL;DR**
> - Undervolting reduces CPU voltage without reducing clock speed — same FPS, lower temps
> - **AMD Ryzen (AM5/AM4):** Use PBO Curve Optimizer in BIOS — start with -15 all-core, test, then push to -25 or -30 per core
> - **Intel (LGA 1851/1700):** Use Intel XTU or BIOS voltage offset — start with -50mV, test, then push to -80mV or -100mV
> - Expect 10–25°C cooler temps and 3–8 dB quieter fans with zero FPS loss
> - Always stress test after each change — Cinebench R24 + 30 minutes of your heaviest game
> - Run your build through our [free bottleneck analyzer](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store) to check if thermal throttling is creating a CPU bottleneck

## Why Undervolting Matters for Gaming Performance

Modern CPUs use aggressive boost algorithms — AMD's Precision Boost Overdrive and Intel's Thermal Velocity Boost — that push clock speeds as high as thermals allow. The relationship is direct: lower temperatures mean the CPU sustains higher boost clocks for longer, which translates to better gaming performance during extended sessions.

Here's what actually happens in a typical gaming scenario without undervolting:

1. You launch a demanding game like The Witcher 4 or Marvel Rivals
2. The CPU boosts to its maximum frequency (say, 5.7 GHz on a Ryzen 7 9800X3D)
3. After 10–20 minutes, temperatures climb to 90–95°C
4. The boost algorithm starts pulling back clocks to stay within thermal limits
5. Your effective frequency drops to 5.3–5.5 GHz — a 3–7% performance loss you never see reported in reviews

This is thermal throttling, and it's the hidden bottleneck that most monitoring overlays don't flag clearly. Reviews test with open benches and high-end cooling — your closed case with a mid-range cooler tells a different story.

Undervolting attacks this problem at the source. By reducing voltage to only what the silicon actually needs, you break the temperature-throttle cycle and keep your CPU at full boost throughout the entire gaming session.

### What Undervolting Is NOT

Let's clear up confusion that keeps people from trying this:

- **It's not overclocking.** You're not increasing frequency or pushing beyond spec. You're reducing waste.
- **It's not dangerous.** Too little voltage causes a crash or reboot — it cannot damage your CPU. The worst case is a system restart, and you try a less aggressive setting.
- **It's not voiding your warranty.** AMD and Intel both provide software tools for voltage adjustment. PBO Curve Optimizer is a supported BIOS feature, and Intel XTU is Intel's own application.
- **It's not unstable.** A properly tested undervolt is as stable as stock settings — many are *more* stable because reduced heat means less thermal stress on the silicon.

## AMD Ryzen Undervolting: PBO Curve Optimizer Guide

AMD's Curve Optimizer is the most effective CPU undervolting tool available in 2026. Rather than applying a flat voltage offset, it adjusts the voltage-frequency curve on a per-core basis, letting you dial in the optimal voltage for each core's individual silicon quality.

### Supported CPUs

Curve Optimizer works on all Ryzen 5000, 7000, and 9000 series processors:

- **AM5 (Ryzen 9000/7000):** Ryzen 5 9600X, Ryzen 7 9700X, Ryzen 7 9800X3D, Ryzen 9 9900X, Ryzen 9 9950X, Ryzen 7 7800X3D, Ryzen 5 7600X, and all other AM5 chips
- **AM4 (Ryzen 5000):** Ryzen 5 5600X, Ryzen 7 5800X3D, Ryzen 9 5900X, Ryzen 9 5950X, and all other Zen 3 chips

### Step-by-Step: Curve Optimizer Setup

**Step 1: Enter BIOS and navigate to PBO settings**

Restart your PC and enter BIOS (usually Delete or F2 during boot). Navigate to:
- **ASUS:** AI Tweaker → Precision Boost Overdrive → Curve Optimizer
- **MSI:** OC → Advanced CPU Configuration → AMD Overclocking → PBO → Curve Optimizer
- **Gigabyte:** Tweaker → Advanced CPU Settings → PBO → Curve Optimizer Control
- **ASRock:** OC Tweaker → CPU Configuration → PBO → Curve Optimizer

**Step 2: Set to All Core, Negative offset**

- Set Curve Optimizer to **All Core**
- Set the sign to **Negative**
- Start with a magnitude of **15**

This tells the CPU to use 15 "counts" less voltage across its entire voltage-frequency curve. Each count is roughly 3–5mV depending on the operating point, so -15 is approximately a 45–75mV reduction.

**Step 3: Save, reboot, and test stability**

Boot into Windows and run:
1. **Cinebench R24** — multi-core test, 10-minute loop. If it completes without crashing, basic stability is confirmed.
2. **Your heaviest game** — play for at least 30 minutes. CPU-heavy titles like Cities: Skylines 2 or Civilization VII are ideal tests.
3. **CoreCycler** (optional) — this free tool stress-tests each core individually, which catches per-core instability that all-core tests miss.

**Step 4: Push further**

If -15 is stable, go back to BIOS and increase to -20. Test again. Then -25, then -30. Most Ryzen 7000/9000 chips can handle -20 to -30 all-core. The sweet spot for most gaming CPUs:

| CPU | Typical Stable All-Core Offset | Expected Temp Drop |
|---|---|---|
| <AffiliateLink name="AMD Ryzen 9 9950X" /> | -20 to -30 | 15–22°C |
| <AffiliateLink name="AMD Ryzen 7 9800X3D" /> | -15 to -25 | 10–18°C |
| <AffiliateLink name="AMD Ryzen 7 9700X" /> | -20 to -30 | 12–20°C |
| <AffiliateLink name="AMD Ryzen 5 9600X" /> | -25 to -35 | 10–15°C |
| <AffiliateLink name="AMD Ryzen 7 7800X3D" /> | -20 to -30 | 12–20°C |
| <AffiliateLink name="AMD Ryzen 5 7600X" /> | -25 to -35 | 10–18°C |

**Step 5 (Advanced): Per-core tuning**

Once you find your all-core limit, switch Curve Optimizer to **Per Core** mode. Your best cores (marked with a star in Ryzen Master or HWiNFO) may tolerate less offset (-10 to -15), while your weaker cores can be pushed harder (-30 to -40). This maximizes both single-thread boost performance and multi-thread efficiency.

## Intel CPU Undervolting: Voltage Offset Guide

Intel's approach to undervolting is different from AMD's. Instead of a curve optimizer, you apply a flat voltage offset that reduces the voltage at every operating point by a fixed amount.

### Supported CPUs

- **LGA 1851 (Arrow Lake):** Core Ultra 9 285K, Core Ultra 7 265K, Core Ultra 5 245K — fully supported
- **LGA 1700 (Raptor Lake/Alder Lake):** Core i9-14900K, Core i7-14700K, Core i5-14600K, and all 12th/13th-gen K-series — fully supported
- **Non-K models:** Limited undervolting support varies by motherboard vendor. Some B-series boards lock voltage adjustment; Z-series boards typically allow it even on non-K chips.

### Method 1: Intel XTU (Software — Easiest)

Intel Extreme Tuning Utility is the simplest path for Intel undervolting:

1. Download Intel XTU from Intel's website
2. Open XTU and navigate to the **Core** tab
3. Find **Core Voltage Offset** and set it to **-50mV**
4. Click **Apply** — the change takes effect immediately, no reboot needed
5. Run Cinebench R24 for a quick stability check
6. If stable, increase to -60mV, then -70mV, testing each time
7. Once you find the crash point, back off by 10mV — that's your daily setting

### Method 2: BIOS Voltage Offset (More Reliable)

For a persistent undervolt that applies from boot:

1. Enter BIOS (Delete or F2 during boot)
2. Navigate to CPU voltage settings:
   - **ASUS:** AI Tweaker → CPU Core Voltage → Offset Mode → Set negative offset
   - **MSI:** OC → CPU Core Voltage → Set to Offset Mode → Enter negative value
   - **Gigabyte:** Tweaker → CPU Vcore → Set to Offset → Enter negative value
3. Start at -50mV and increase from there

> **Important for 13th/14th Gen Intel owners:** The well-documented Raptor Lake degradation issue means undervolting is especially important for these chips. Reducing voltage lowers the electrical stress that contributes to long-term instability. If you're running a 13900K or 14900K, undervolting isn't just a nice-to-have — it's a recommended protective measure. ## Troubleshooting: What to Do When It Crashes

An undervolt-related crash is harmless — it just means you pushed too far. Here's how to handle each scenario:

### System won't boot after BIOS undervolt
Most motherboards have a "last known good" recovery: power off, hold the power button for 10 seconds, or clear CMOS by removing the motherboard battery for 30 seconds. This resets BIOS to defaults, and you can re-enter with a less aggressive offset.

### Crashes during stress testing
Back off by 5 counts (AMD) or 10mV (Intel) from the crashing value. Some instability only appears in specific workloads — heavy AVX instructions are more voltage-sensitive than gaming, so you can sometimes use a more aggressive undervolt for daily gaming than for all-core rendering.

### Stable in benchmarks, crashes in games
This usually means per-core instability. One core is hitting its limit while others are fine. On AMD, switch to per-core tuning and find the weak core. On Intel, back off the global offset by 10mV.

### Random crashes weeks later
Temperature and silicon characteristics change slightly with seasons and silicon aging. If a previously stable undervolt starts causing issues, back off by 5–10mV. This is rare but normal.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does undervolting reduce CPU lifespan?

No. Undervolting reduces electrical stress on the silicon, which if anything extends the CPU's usable lifespan. The concern would be with *over*volting, which is the opposite operation.

### Can I undervolt a laptop CPU?

Yes, but support varies by manufacturer. Intel laptop CPUs often support undervolting through Throttlestop (a third-party tool). AMD laptop APUs have more limited options — some support through Ryzen Controller. Check our [laptop optimization guide](/blog/how-to-optimize-gaming-laptop-for-max-fps-fix-throttling-bottlenecks-2026) for details.

### Should I undervolt my 3D V-Cache CPU?

Absolutely. The Ryzen 7 7800X3D and 9800X3D benefit significantly from undervolting because they already run at AMD's thermal limits. A -20 to -25 offset is typically stable and drops temps by 12–20°C, which helps the 3D V-Cache stay at its optimal operating temperature.

### Does undervolting affect XMP/EXPO memory profiles?

No. CPU core voltage and memory voltage are completely independent. Your RAM will continue running at its XMP/EXPO speed regardless of your CPU undervolt settings.

### What's the difference between undervolting and lowering power limits?

Lowering power limits (PBO power limit tuning on AMD, PL1/PL2 on Intel) restricts how much power the CPU can draw, which *does* reduce performance in multi-core workloads. Undervolting reduces the voltage-to-frequency ratio without limiting power draw, so performance stays the same while heat output drops. Undervolting is the superior approach for gaming.
