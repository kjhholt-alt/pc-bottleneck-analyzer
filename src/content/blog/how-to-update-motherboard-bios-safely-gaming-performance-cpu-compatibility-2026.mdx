# How to Update Your Motherboard BIOS Safely: Fix CPU Bottlenecks, RAM Speed, and Gaming Performance (2026)

Your motherboard's BIOS is the most overlooked piece of software in your gaming PC. It's the firmware that runs before Windows even loads — it initializes your CPU, sets your RAM speed, configures your PCIe lanes, and manages power delivery. And if it's outdated, it can be the silent reason your system isn't performing the way it should.

We've analyzed tens of thousands of system scans through our [PC Bottleneck Analyzer](https://pcbottleneck.buildkit.store), and one pattern comes up repeatedly: users with capable hardware leaving 5–15% of their performance on the table because their BIOS is three or four revisions behind. Outdated AGESA or microcode means their CPU boosts less aggressively, their RAM won't hit rated speeds, or their system randomly crashes under gaming load.

This guide walks you through exactly when to update, how to do it safely on every major motherboard brand, and what performance gains to expect.

> - A BIOS update is the single most important step after building a new PC or upgrading your CPU

## When You Should (and Shouldn't) Update Your BIOS

Not every BIOS update is worth installing. Here's a clear decision framework:

### You SHOULD update if:

**You just installed a new CPU.** This is the single most important reason. New CPUs often require BIOS updates to function at all. For example, AMD's Ryzen 9000 series processors needed AGESA 1.2.0.0 or later on AM5 boards — if your B650 motherboard shipped with an earlier version, a Ryzen 9 9900X wouldn't even POST. Similarly, Intel's Arrow Lake refresh chips on LGA 1851 required specific microcode updates to boot.

**Your RAM won't hit its rated speed.** If you bought DDR5-6000 CL30 RAM and XMP/EXPO won't apply or causes crashes, there's a very high chance a BIOS update fixes it. RAM compatibility improves dramatically with AGESA and microcode updates. We've seen boards go from supporting DDR5-5200 maximum to stable DDR5-8000 across three BIOS revisions.

**You're experiencing random crashes or BSODs under gaming load.** Stability patches are a major component of BIOS updates. AMD's AGESA updates in 2025–2026 fixed dozens of edge-case stability issues with Zen 5 processors under mixed workloads. Intel's microcode updates addressed voltage regulation issues that caused crashes at stock settings.

**The changelog specifically mentions performance improvements.** Some BIOS updates include significant performance fixes. AMD's AGESA 1.2.0.2 update for Ryzen 9000 series, for example, improved gaming performance by 5–8% in certain titles by optimizing the Zen 5 branch predictor behavior through microcode.

**You're having USB, audio, or PCIe device issues.** BIOS updates frequently fix peripheral compatibility problems that no amount of driver reinstallation will solve.

### You should NOT update if:

**Everything is running perfectly.** The old wisdom "if it ain't broke, don't fix it" has some truth. If your system is stable, your RAM hits its rated speed, and your gaming performance matches benchmarks, there's limited upside.

**The update changelog only mentions "minor bug fixes" or features you don't use.** Updating for the sake of updating introduces a small but nonzero risk of new issues.

**You're on a very old motherboard with no BIOS flashback.** Boards from 2018 or earlier without a dual BIOS or recovery mechanism carry higher risk if something goes wrong during the flash.

## What You Need Before You Start

Before touching your BIOS, gather these items:

### 1. Your exact motherboard model

Not your chipset — your specific board model. "B650" isn't enough. You need "ASUS ROG STRIX B650E-E GAMING WIFI" or "MSI MAG X870 TOMAHAWK WIFI." The easiest way to find this:

- **Windows:** Open Command Prompt and type `wmic baseboard get product,manufacturer`
- **In BIOS:** It's displayed on the main screen when you enter BIOS
- **Physically:** Printed on the board itself between the PCIe slots

### 2. A USB drive (FAT32 formatted)

Most BIOS updates require a USB flash drive formatted as FAT32. Use a small drive (2–32 GB) — larger drives sometimes cause issues with BIOS flash utilities. Here's how to format it:

1. Insert the USB drive
2. Open File Explorer, right-click the drive
3. Select Format → File system: FAT32 → Quick Format → Start
4. This erases everything on the drive, so back up any files first

### 3. The correct BIOS file

Go directly to your motherboard manufacturer's support page:

- **ASUS:** [asus.com/support](https://asus.com/support) → Enter your board model → Driver & Utility → BIOS & Firmware
- **MSI:** [msi.com/support](https://msi.com/support) → Enter your board model → Download → BIOS
- **Gigabyte:** [gigabyte.com/support](https://gigabyte.com/support) → Enter your board model → BIOS
- **ASRock:** [asrock.com/support](https://asrock.com/support) → Enter your board model → BIOS

Download the latest BIOS version (or the specific version you're targeting). The file is typically a ZIP archive containing a single `.CAP` (ASUS), `.ROM` (MSI), or similar BIOS file.

**Critical: Only download BIOS files from your motherboard manufacturer's official website. Never from forums, file sharing sites, or third-party driver tools.** A corrupted or wrong BIOS file can brick your board.

### 4. Stable power

A BIOS flash takes 3–7 minutes. If power cuts out during the process, you can corrupt the BIOS. If your area has unreliable power, consider using a UPS. At minimum, make sure no one is going to trip over your power cable during the update.

## How to Update BIOS: Step-by-Step for Every Major Brand

The process varies slightly by manufacturer, but the core workflow is the same. We'll cover the two main methods: updating through BIOS itself (recommended) and USB BIOS Flashback (for when you can't even boot).

### Method 1: Update Through BIOS (Recommended)

This is the standard method when your system boots normally.

#### ASUS (EZ Flash)

1. Extract the downloaded BIOS ZIP file and copy the `.CAP` file to your FAT32 USB drive's root directory
2. Restart your PC and enter BIOS by pressing **Delete** or **F2** during boot
3. Navigate to **Tool** → **ASUS EZ Flash 3 Utility**
4. Select the USB drive and highlight the `.CAP` file
5. Click **Update** and confirm
6. Wait 3–5 minutes — the system will reboot automatically
7. Enter BIOS again after reboot and load optimized defaults (**F5**)

#### MSI (M-Flash)

1. Extract the BIOS file and copy it to your FAT32 USB drive
2. Restart and enter BIOS by pressing **Delete**
3. Navigate to **M-Flash** (usually top-right of the BIOS screen)
4. The system reboots into M-Flash mode automatically
5. Select the BIOS file from your USB drive
6. Confirm the update and wait 3–7 minutes
7. After reboot, enter BIOS and load optimized defaults

#### Gigabyte (Q-Flash)

1. Copy the extracted BIOS file to your FAT32 USB drive
2. Restart and press **Delete** to enter BIOS
3. Press **F8** or navigate to **Q-Flash** in the BIOS menu
4. Select **Update BIOS**
5. Choose the BIOS file from your USB drive
6. Confirm and wait for the process to complete (3–5 minutes)
7. Load optimized defaults after reboot

#### ASRock (Instant Flash)

1. Copy the extracted BIOS file to your FAT32 USB drive
2. Restart and enter BIOS by pressing **Delete** or **F2**
3. Navigate to **Tool** → **Instant Flash**
4. The utility automatically detects the BIOS file on your USB drive
5. Select the file and confirm the update
6. Wait for completion and automatic reboot
7. Enter BIOS and load optimized defaults

### Method 2: USB BIOS Flashback (No CPU Required)

This is the method you use when:
- Your new CPU isn't recognized by the current BIOS
- Your system won't POST at all
- You've bricked your BIOS and need to recover

Most modern AM5 and LGA 1851 motherboards (B650, X670, X870, B860, Z890) include a BIOS Flashback button on the rear I/O panel. Here's how:

1. Download the BIOS file and extract it
2. **Rename the file** according to your manufacturer's requirement:
   - **ASUS:** Rename to the specific name listed in your board's manual (often the board model name `.CAP`)
   - **MSI:** Rename to `MSI.ROM`
   - **Gigabyte:** Rename to `GIGABYTE.bin`
   - **ASRock:** Check your manual — varies by model
3. Copy the renamed file to the root of a FAT32 USB drive
4. Plug the USB drive into the **BIOS Flashback USB port** (usually marked on the rear I/O, often a specific USB 2.0 port — check your manual)
5. With the PC **powered off but plugged in**, press and hold the BIOS Flashback button for 3 seconds
6. The button's LED starts blinking — this means the flash is in progress
7. Wait until the LED stops blinking (3–8 minutes). **Do not unplug power or remove the USB drive**
8. Once the LED goes off, power on normally

This method works even with no CPU, no RAM, and no GPU installed. It's purely a motherboard firmware operation, which makes it the safest recovery option and the only way to prepare a board for a CPU it doesn't natively support.

## Critical Step Most People Skip: Load Optimized Defaults After Updating

After any BIOS update, your first action should be entering BIOS and pressing **F5** (or the equivalent key for your board) to load optimized defaults. Here's why this matters:

**BIOS updates change memory training algorithms.** Your old RAM settings may not carry over cleanly. Loading defaults forces the board to retrain memory from scratch, which prevents instability.

**Power delivery profiles change.** Updated AGESA or microcode may use different voltage curves. Old settings can conflict with new power management behavior, causing your CPU to boost incorrectly or draw too much power.

**New features may require fresh configuration.** Features like Precision Boost Overdrive 2, Curve Optimizer on AMD, or Intel's APO (Application Performance Optimizer) may not work correctly with carried-over settings.

After loading defaults:
1. Re-enable XMP/EXPO for your RAM
2. Re-apply any manual overclocks (test stability again — they may need adjustment)
3. Set your preferred fan curves
4. Re-enable Resizable BAR if it was turned off
5. Save and exit

## Troubleshooting: What to Do If Something Goes Wrong

### BIOS update freezes at a percentage

**Don't panic. Don't touch anything.** BIOS updates sometimes appear to hang at certain percentages (25% and 90% are common stall points). Give it at least 15 minutes before assuming it's stuck. Most boards have internal timeout mechanisms that will either complete or roll back the update.

### System won't boot after BIOS update

1. **Clear CMOS first.** Turn off the PC, unplug it, and either use the Clear CMOS button on the rear I/O or short the CMOS jumper pins on the motherboard for 10 seconds. This resets all BIOS settings to factory defaults.
2. **Try booting with a single RAM stick.** Memory training after a BIOS update can fail with all sticks installed. Try one stick in the slot recommended by your manual (usually A2).
3. **Use BIOS Flashback** to re-flash the same version or roll back to a previous version.

### BIOS Flashback LED blinks but nothing happens

- Ensure the BIOS file is renamed correctly for your manufacturer
- Try a different USB drive (USB 2.0 drives often work better than USB 3.0)
- Make sure the drive is FAT32, not exFAT or NTFS
- Verify you're using the correct USB port (the BIOS Flashback port, not just any port)

### XMP/EXPO stopped working after BIOS update

This occasionally happens when a new BIOS version changes memory training behavior. Solutions:

1. Load optimized defaults and try applying XMP/EXPO again
2. If it still crashes, manually set the RAM voltage to 1.35V (DDR5) and try again
3. Try one step below your rated speed (e.g., DDR5-5600 instead of DDR5-6000) and work up
4. Check if a newer BIOS version exists that addresses memory compatibility

### You accidentally flashed the wrong BIOS

If you flashed a BIOS meant for a different board model, use BIOS Flashback with the correct file to recover. This is exactly the scenario BIOS Flashback was designed for. Even if the board won't POST, Flashback operates independently of the CPU and can restore the correct firmware.

## Should You Update BIOS Before or After Installing a New CPU?

This is one of the most frequently asked questions in PC building, and the answer depends on your situation:

**Update BEFORE installing the new CPU if:**
- Your current CPU works in the board and you can boot normally
- The new CPU requires a newer BIOS version (check your manufacturer's CPU support list)
- This is the easiest and safest path

**Use BIOS Flashback to update WITHOUT any CPU if:**
- You're building a brand new system
- You don't have an older compatible CPU available
- Your board has BIOS Flashback (most AM5 and LGA 1851 boards do)

**You might get lucky and not need to update if:**
- Your board was manufactured recently and already ships with a recent BIOS
- Check the BIOS version sticker on the motherboard box — some retailers list it

Our recommendation: always update to the latest stable BIOS before installing a new generation CPU. It takes 10 minutes and prevents the frustrating scenario of a new build that won't POST because of a $0 firmware issue.
